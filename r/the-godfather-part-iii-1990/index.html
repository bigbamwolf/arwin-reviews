The Godfather Part III 1990

★★★½
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-02-09
Non spoiler thoughts (summary): This feels like a story about legacy, aging, and the heavy cost of power. I’m drawn to how the characters carry history on their shoulders, especially Michael trying to clean up a life that refuses to stay buried. The performances give the film an emotional weight that’s less about crime and more about inheritance, guilt, and family. For me, it plays like a reflection on what happens when ambition outlives the man who built it, and how maturity doesn’t erase consequence. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you’ve seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ The first thing that came to my mind was what the F is that hair? And why do I get so attracted to Connie as she gets older? There’s something about her presence here that feels more caring and confident, and I found myself oddly drawn to that maturity. I laughed when Michael mentioned having a temper like his father while referring to Sonny and Vincent. And it’s true, Andy Garcia really does channel James Caan. I see the same aggressiveness, the same explosive energy. Vincent feels like a spiritual continuation of Sonny, reckless but driven, which adds tension to every scene he’s in. And who can forget Joe Mantegna’s face after terrorizing the screen in Baby’s Day Out? That childhood villain image still sticks with me. Seeing him here gives that same uneasy vibe, and Vincent’s revenge against him felt carefully planned and satisfying. Deserve! This installment leans more toward politics and legacy, focusing on the passing of the baton to the newer generation. I feel the film is less about street level crime, violence and more about power, image, and the burden of inheritance. Michael is clearly trying to legitimize everything, but the past keeps pulling him back. “Never hate your enemies, it will cloud your judgment.” That line stayed with me. It perfectly summarizes Michael’s philosophy at this stage, a wiser man compared from Part II. He’s colder, more reflective, and trying to operate above emotion, even when the stakes are deeply personal. The timing of Michael’s confession followed by the Pope’s death hit me symbolically. Both figures act as fathers in their own realms, and I read it as the death of Michael’s old identity as Godfather too. He’s exhausted, spiritually broken, and clearly done with the cycle of violence, even if the world around him won’t let him walk away clean. The sequence after the opera is insanely well directed and acted. I genuinely felt Michael’s pain when he lost his daughter. That silent scream is devastating. When Connie put on the black veil, I immediately thought about how the opera itself foreshadowed tragedy. The staging, the intercut violence, and the emotional payoff all came together in a way that felt grand and cruel at the same time. Overall, it’s about a man trying to outrun the sins of his empire and realizing too late that power always demands a price.
