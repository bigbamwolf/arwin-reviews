Punch-Drunk Love 2002

★★★
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-01-12
What is up with PTA suddenly throwing in jump scares? Haha. And the lens flares too. PTA, are you trying to copy J.J. Abrams? The long uninterrupted takes, a clear PTA DNA, are very evident here. He really uses the entire space, letting the characters move freely and exist inside the frame instead of cutting around them. It makes the performances feel more raw and lived-in. At first, this film feels like it’s going to be about isolation and loneliness, and that assumption holds throughout. The musical score has the same looping, atmospheric quality found in his other films, almost syncing with the visuals and Barry’s internal rhythm. Barry, played by Adam Sandler, is awkward, math-savvy, mentally unstable, and painfully lonely. PTA frames him using silhouettes and strong leading lines, visually reinforcing how boxed-in and overwhelmed he feels. It’s interesting to notice that PTA already used the car door closing shot here, something he later reused in One Battle After Another after the kidnapping of Chase Infiniti. You can really see ideas forming and evolving across his films. It’s also refreshing to see Philip Seymour Hoffman play a serious, aggressive antagonist, similar to his role in Mission: Impossible III. I honestly grew tired of him being cast by PTA as a mediocre or side character, like the gay comedic relief in Boogie Nights or the nurse in Magnolia. Here, he feels dangerous. By the last act, Adam Sandler starts giving off strong Howard Ratner energy from Uncut Gems, frantic, impulsive, and emotionally explosive. Knowing what Sandler can do now, this performance feels like an early blueprint of that intensity. I honestly thought that he was hallucinating when he single handedly squashed his attackers. Some kind of illusion. But it turns out it was real. I think that because of love, he already knows how to compartmentalize his aggression, and now it is directed toward those who hurt his love interest. The shifting colors feel like title cards per act, but I’m not very adept at reading the meaning of colors. That’s why I wasn’t able to fully connect with what PTA was trying to say through them, but it’s still a nice touch. Overall, this film feels like an experimental playground for PTA. A space where he tests new visual ideas, tonal shifts, and narrative risks, honing skills that would later define his more confident and complex works.
