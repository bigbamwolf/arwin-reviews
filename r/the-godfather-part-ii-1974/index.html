The Godfather Part II 1974

★★★★
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-02-05
Non spoiler thoughts (summary): The Godfather Part II shifts away from loud violence into quiet power plays, where conversations, looks, and decisions carry more danger than gunfire. It’s structured like a chess match across two timelines, contrasting the rise of one man with the moral collapse of another. Thematically dense, this isn’t just a continuation, it’s a tragic deepening of everything the first film set up. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you’ve seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ I already hear the intense shout of Al Pacino here when he ordered “Rocco, alive!” after his attempted assassination. After seven years of being the Godfather, Michael is totally different now. Also the moment with Kay, when she tells him about the abortion, is especially terrifying. I can see that Michael wants to strangle Kay at that very moment. I was surprised to see Robert De Niro as young Vito Corleone. Buti na lang napanood ko ito after Heat, because otherwise it would feel weird seeing the father and son eventually kill each other in other film. De Niro’s Vito is quiet, observant, and patient. He builds power slowly, methodically, and always with purpose. His rise feels earned, almost noble in contrast to Michael’s descent. This film feels more like quiet power plays, parang giant chess match. It’s a stark difference from the first film, which felt louder and more openly violent. Here, the danger is in conversations, and decisions made behind closed doors. Every move has long term consequences. Visually, the film reinforces this emotional shift. Most of the dialogue involving power and threats is lit from above, only lighting the head while casting deep shadows from the forehead down to the eyes. This makes the characters look brooding, mythic, and inhuman. They feel less like men and more like symbols of power without conscience or empathy. Props to the production design and wardrobe for clearly distinguishing the two eras as the film jumps back and forth in time. The contrast between young Vito’s rise and Michael’s rule is not just visual but thematic. Both men are powerful, ambitious, and vengeful at similar ages. But Vito builds his empire by strengthening family ties, while Michael isolates himself due to paranoia. Vito keeps his family together. Michael becomes a lone wolf. For that reason, for me, Vito is the better Godfather. He understood that power without family is hollow. Michael, despite having absolute control, ends up completely alone. This is not just a sequel. It’s a tragedy unfolding in parallel timelines, showing how two men can walk similar paths and still end up in completely opposite places.
