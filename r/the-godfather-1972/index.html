The Godfather (1972) review

★★★★½
Rated 4.5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-02-02
Crime, Drama · Directed by Francis Ford Coppola · 175 min
607 reviews · 1,533 films logged · 157,261 words
Non spoiler thoughts (summary): This watch clicked for me in a way it didn’t before. Understanding the film’s language and power dynamics makes everything richer. The quiet rules of loyalty, favors, and obligation are what truly drive the story, and the film communicates that without spelling it out. Performances are controlled and restrained, especially Al Pacino’s, whose presence evolves subtly but powerfully. Coppola’s use of silence, sound, and pacing is masterful, turning small moments into defining ones. It’s a film that rewards patience and attention, and once it locks in, it becomes impossible to forget. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you’ve seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ I enjoyed this far more than the last time I tried to finish it. This time around, I understood the cinema language and the power dynamics much better. You don’t say no to a mafia boss, and the film clearly shows how favors, loyalty, and money quietly shape politics and society behind closed doors. Young Robert Duvall surprisingly reminded me of Harvey Specter in Suits. Al Pacino here is very different from the intensity of Vincent in Heat. He’s young, restrained, almost invisible at first. Calm, graceful, and reserved. But by the end, his character development completely flips the dynamic. He’s still controlled and calculated, but now there’s authority in his words and gaze. It really embodies the idea that you should never mistake kindness for weakness. There’s also a beautiful romantic score during Michael’s time with the Sicilian girl. This was actually the part where I stopped watching before, so everything after that felt like new territory for me. It made the second half of the film much more surprising and engaging. Talia Shire is strikingly beautiful here as Connie. I first knew her as Adrian in Rocky, but her performance during the domestic violence scene is intense and uncomfortable, in a way that feels painfully real. One thing that didn’t work for me is the exaggerated acting during some of the shooting scenes. Characters like Vito and Sonny still reacting even after being riddled with bullets felt over the top. One or two shots should have been enough for them to drop cold. That said, this is more a product of its time than a deal breaker. Francis Ford Coppola’s use of silence is masterful. Silence becomes the atmosphere of conversations, and when sound does come in, it’s always purposeful. Michael’s hesitation before killing Sollozzo is amplified by the sound of a train approaching, signaling the moment his decision hardens. During the baptism sequence, the swelling score, the priest’s Latin dialogue, and the cries of the baby collide with the assassinations of the rival families. It’s chilling and brilliant. Then there’s Kay confronting Michael about Carlo’s death, with the ticking clock in the background. To me, it signals the moment Michael fully crosses the threshold into his new life. The final visuals are just as powerful. Connie in the foreground, Michael in the background, framed as if trapped by his new role. He feels small, boxed in by responsibility, and already lying to the woman he loves. When the capos kiss his hand and call him Godfather, it’s the perfect closing note. Chef’s kiss. A truly iconic ending.
