Oldboy 2003

★★★
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-02-06
Non spoiler thoughts (summary): Knowing the twist going in changes the experience completely. Instead of shock, the film becomes about tone, style, and performance. This version leans heavily into stylization, mood, and surreal pacing, making it feel more like psychological horror than a straight revenge thriller. Even without the surprise factor, it’s interesting to see how the same premise can be interpreted through a more artistic lens, creating a tense, uneasy watch driven by atmosphere rather than plot mechanics. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you’ve seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ I watched this already knowing the big twist because I saw the American remake with Josh Brolin and Elizabeth Olsen first, and that version seriously mind fucked me. So coming into this, I wasn’t exactly excited. I was hoping to get something new out of the experience rather than just reliving the shock. Right away, the biggest difference is tone and style. This version feels far more artistic in color, camera movement, and score. Whereas the remake plays more like a straightforward revenge thriller with a plot twist at the center. The Korean film leans into mood and stylization, letting scenes breathe in a surreal way. Watching the sex scene with the knowledge of the twist is deeply disturbing and disgusting. There’s no shock anymore, just dread and that makes the scene harder to sit through. Performance wise, I didn’t connect much with the main character here. Choi Min-sik’s portrayal looks like a comedian with the wild hair and manic breakdown. He does not look like someone who carries out a vengeful act. Compared to Josh Brolin’s version, which leans more into guilt, rage, and psychological damage, this take feels hysterical and oddly funny at times instead of tragic. Lesson learned: don’t be a peeping tom and don’t fuck with someone twice younger than yourself. I actually prefer how the remake handles the incest twist. Making it father and daughter adds an extra layer of karmic cruelty and long term planning. The TV revelation being designed specifically for Josh Brolin’s character isolating him with the truth feels colder and more calculated. It reinforces how the revenge wasn’t impulsive, but engineered for maximum psychological destruction over decades. It also makes narrative sense that Mia is positioned to care for him, instead of him randomly waking up with someone after blacking out after an hypnosis. The endings also diverge emotionally. Here, he chooses ignorance wishing not to know, yet the pain in his eyes suggests memory still lingers beneath the hypnosis. The remake’s ending feels more like self punishment and accountability, with the character imprisoning himself again as a twisted form of repentance. Despite sharing the same core premise, these films are fundamentally different in execution and intention. The remake isn’t trying to copy word for word, because where's the fun in that? The remake reshapes the material through a different cinematic language and moral framing. In the end, I think it’s unfair to judge one strictly against the other. They stand as separate interpretations, one leaning into stylized psychological horror, the other into cold, calculated revenge tragedy. Watching both highlights how the same narrative skeleton can produce wildly different cinematic experiences.
