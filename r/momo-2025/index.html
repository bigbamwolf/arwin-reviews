Momo (2025) review

★★★½
Rated 3.5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-02-05
620 reviews · 1,546 films logged · 160,269 words
Non spoiler thoughts (summary): The time concept alone is instantly intriguing. A futuristic world where time feels like currency sets up a clean, sci-fi playground that blends sleek aesthetics with philosophical undertones. Even without diving into plot specifics, the film clearly wants you thinking about how modern life pressures people to optimize every second instead of actually living. The pacing has some quieter stretches, but the atmosphere, production design, and central theme keep it engaging. What starts like a kid friendly adventure slowly reveals itself as a thoughtful reflection on presence, pressure, and what it really means to use your time well. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you’ve seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ The time concept alone got me curious enough to watch this. It feels like In Time with the idea of time as currency and lifeline, mixed with the clean futuristic aesthetic of A Wrinkle in Time or Tomorrowland. Upon watching, there’s even a Tenet nuance because of the reverse mechanics of time elements. The bracelet commercial early on looks uncanny (looks AI made but I cannot see any article particularly mentioning it). Hindi masyadong sync yung audio sa bibig, the texture of the skin looks rubbery, the movement, and emotion feels robotic. That discomfort works in the film’s favor because it mirrors the artificiality of a world obsessed with optimization and efficiency over humanity. Very early, I started seeing the bracelet not as a symbol of freedom, but as a guilt trip device. Instead of helping people live better, it pressures them into constantly thinking about the future. It feels less like empowerment and more like a shackle. You’re not enjoying the present because you’re always measuring what comes next. That train of thought becomes one of the film’s strongest themes. What really hits is the line: “They rush and fret and stress but still somehow bored.” That perfectly sums up adulthood. People are overstimulated yet emotionally empty. There’s a clear Le Petit Prince parallel here: the idea that modern life strips away wonder. The Contacts system reinforces that commentary, especially when it promotes reading messages while watching a movie. It’s overstimulation packaged as productivity. Another smart visual cue is how the bracelet glows red whenever characters form emotional connections. The system treats feelings as wasted time, when in reality those moments are what make us human. The film quietly argues that emotional presence is not inefficiency, but it’s meaning. I almost drifted off during parts of the second act because the pacing slows down, but the third act snaps everything into urgency. It becomes a literal race against time for Momo, the time thieves, and Master Hora. The swelling score pushes that tension forward, making the climax feel deserved. At its core, this is one of those stories where a child stands against a system built by villain adults (like Ender's Game, Stranger Things, Spy Kids, etc). I initially expected something childish and lightweight, but it turns into a thoughtful, thrilling reflection on how we treat time. The final message lands clean: How you use time is entirely up to you, so enjoy it because we only have now. It works both as advice for kids learning to shape their future and as a reminder for adults to stop living only in anticipation. The film ultimately argues that time isn’t something to hoard or optimize, it’s something to experience. A surprisingly warm and philosophical ride disguised as a futuristic adventure.
