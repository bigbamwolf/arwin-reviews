The Son (2022) review

★★★
Rated 3/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-11-06
655 reviews · 1,581 films logged · 175,968 words
I understood the depression. I just never believed the performance. I get the problems. That is also why I really don’t think I would want a son. I would understand my girls if they had the same problems, but I don’t think I am capable of empathizing as easily with a son who has a weak mental and emotional fortitude. I would be really frustrated with a lying teenager. Especially with this look like a suicidal twink. I understand depression intellectually, but watching someone repeatedly lie, withdraw, and refuse to deal with what is happening around him makes it difficult for me to connect emotionally. I like the visual representation of the washing machine. The intensity of the spinning feels like a representation of how hard the turmoil in their lives has become. Everything keeps turning, but nobody is actually getting anywhere. I get the whole trauma being passed down. The abandonment issues. The expectations placed on men. The idea that men are supposed to endure things quietly and just deal with life. Peter grew up emotionally disconnected from his own father, and now he is trying to be a different kind of father without really knowing how. But I just don’t know. You can hate me all you want, but I will really have a hard time comprehending weak men who are not capable of dealing with life. That is probably also why Nicholas never completely worked for me emotionally. I like Hugh Jackman and Vanessa Kirby’s acting, but I am not very particular about Zen McGrath and Laura Dern. Vanessa Kirby really sells the frustration of getting tangled in all of this even though she is only the stepmom. She can already see that Nicholas is not well, and there is something frustrating about watching her try to convince Peter while the problem keeps getting bigger. Hugh Jackman is much stronger. I can feel the guilt, frustration, helplessness, and the constant pressure of trying to fix something he does not fully understand. His gradual realization that career success means nothing if he loses his family gives him the strongest character arc in the film. I like his character development, even if he never really got a sense of closure with his own father. He cannot fix what happened between him and his dad, but at least he tries to stop repeating the same emotional distance with his own family. I still prefer Beautiful Boy (2018), storywise and acting wise. That film made me understand both the father and the son. Timothée Chalamet made Nic’s desperation, addiction, shame, and helplessness feel real. Zen McGrath is way, way below Timothée’s acting capabilities for me. Even during intense moments, Zen’s acting becomes laughable. His pain and desperation are not believable. It feels like I am watching someone perform sadness instead of actually watching a teenager who is drowning. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you've seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ I mean, it is a sad conclusion, but the ending is too predictable. I already knew he was going to commit suicide after being released. And they were so stupid to become complacent about putting dangerous things away from him. They should have known better, especially with the hunting rifle. After everything they already knew about his mental state, leaving something like that accessible frustrated me more than it shocked me. But the daydreaming scene about what could have been got me. I teared up seeing the life Peter imagined his son could have had. Growing older. Having a future. Becoming someone beyond the pain they were currently living through. As a father who lost two children, I sometimes think about the what could have been too. What would life be like if they were still with us? What would they look like going to school? There was also a time when I blamed myself for what happened to them. Thank God He healed me from that pain. No parent deserves to see their child or children die. Hugh Jackman's performance and that devastating imagined future sequence are the saving graces of this film. Without them, especially that final sequence, I would have rated this much lower because Zen McGrath's performance, the predictable tragedy, and my lack of emotional connection with Nicholas really held it back. Verdict: Watchable Watch on Prime TL;DR: I understand the film’s exploration of depression, generational trauma, abandonment, and the pressure placed on men, but I had a hard time emotionally connecting with Nicholas, especially because Zen McGrath’s performance never felt convincing to me. Hugh Jackman and Vanessa Kirby were much stronger, and I particularly liked Peter’s struggle to become a better father despite his own unresolved issues. I still prefer Beautiful Boy (2018), both in storytelling and performances. But despite my frustrations with the film, one deeply emotional moment hit me personally as a father who has lost two children. I teared up because I know what it feels like to wonder about the life your children could have had.
