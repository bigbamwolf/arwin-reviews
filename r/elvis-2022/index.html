Elvis 2022

★★★★½
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-02-10
Non spoiler thoughts (summary): This feels like a high energy biopic that translates fame into movement, rhythm, and emotion. I’m pulled in by how the film visualizes Elvis’ rise, not just as success, but as a push and pull between passion and control. The performances and stylistic choices keep everything alive, turning history into something visceral and alive. For me, it plays less like a standard biography and more like a reflection on identity, influence, and the price of being turned into an icon. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you’ve seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ I actually watched about half of this before and only continued now, but honestly I would like to watch it again from the start because the energy hits immediately. The opening is explosive, especially the church scene. It reminds me of Sinners. Nakakaindak talaga. The first performance is incredible in how it translates fanaticism to the screen. The women losing their minds almost feel like they are having an orgasm. The camera movement, editing, and sound design all work together to show how overwhelming Elvis’ presence was. And it is wild to realize this is Feyd Rautha from Dune Part Two. What range! I like the visual effects used to show the map of his tour locations. It turns a simple logistical montage into something dynamic and playful, keeping the pacing alive while emphasizing how massive his rise was. Even though this is a biopic, the lighting and color grading are stylish and expressive. The director constantly translates Elvis’ emotional state into visual metaphors. When Elvis feels lost, the hall of mirrors visually represents confusion and identity fragmentation, while Colonel Parker guiding him through symbolizes control. The ferris wheel scene is even stronger. As Colonel pitches exclusive representation, they are suspended high above everything. Elvis looks unstable and afraid, which mirrors his fear of entering new territory, while the Colonel is calm and confident, clearly holding power. When Elvis agrees and says he is ready to fly, the ferris wheel rises even higher, visually sealing the deal. I love how the film uses space and movement to communicate power dynamics. The portrayal of racial discrimination is striking. Seeing the segregation between white and Black communities adds important context to Elvis’ influences. The film shows how even performance styles were policed, with fear around associating white artists with Black culture. It is uncomfortable but necessary history, and it deepens my understanding of the era Elvis was navigating. The prosthetics and makeup work are impressive. Elvis’ transformation, especially later in life feels convincing. Even his weight gain looks natural. Storytelling wise, I personally find this more engaging than Bohemian Rhapsody. The editing during the concert tour montages keeps the momentum high while reinforcing the intoxicating nature of fame. The film constantly reminds me that success can feel like a cage. Stars are treated less like human beings and more like products, and Elvis’ struggle becomes less about talent and more about ownership and control. By the end, I feel glad I came back to finish this. It is energetic, emotional, and surprisingly intimate beneath all the blinding lights. For me, it becomes a warm story about passion, identity, and the cost of fame. I literally went straight to Spotify to listen to Elvis Presley after watching.
