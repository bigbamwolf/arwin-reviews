Laya (2026) review

★★★★½
Rated 4.5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-11-23
676 reviews · 1,600 films logged · 187,473 words
Apparently the hardest prison to escape is the one you carry with you. This is already my fourth film from director Sigrid Andrea Bernardo, so I know I am in safe hands. After I’mPerfect (2025), I am starting to recognize one of her strengths more clearly: framing. This time, a recurring image is Tuki and Sab framed behind the bars of the windows of Tuki’s rental house. Clear as day visual storytelling. They are not in prison, yet Sigrid keeps visually placing them behind bars because neither of them is actually free from what they are carrying. I also love how she uses height and line of sight to show power dynamics. Who has to look up, and who occupies the stronger position inside the frame already tells me who has control. The scene between Tuki and his resort boss is probably the clearest example. It started as a con job, then I thought it was becoming a revenge film. But as more information comes out, the film slowly becomes something much deeper. Honestly, while watching, I could not shake the feeling that Sab was written with Alessandra de Rossi somewhere in mind. Andrea Brillantes has that strong, boyish, "palamura" energy through the delivery, body language, toughness, and profanity. But Andrea still makes Sab her own. Anthony Jennings is also surprisingly convincing early on as a bad boy despite naturally having such a good boy face. I actually like that contrast because his charm does not become a roadblock. He can still make the character feel suspicious and potentially dangerous without needing to look intimidating. JC Santos is really good at portraying this awkward, kind hearted man with guilt buried deep inside him. He feels like someone who has already changed but still knows that changing does not automatically erase what happened before. At the same time, he is actively trying to build a life that is no longer controlled by his past. And the makeup is top notch! How do you make someone that good looking look this imperfect and undesirable? Mission accomplished! Andrea, JC, and Anthony all acted well, but JC Santos is the highlight for me. I think one of the strongest ideas here is being seen for who you truly are instead of being reduced to the appearance, history, or label people already decided belongs to you. That hits especially hard with Tuki. People can look at his appearance, his criminal record, and his past before they even bother seeing the actual person standing in front of them. The film also briefly touches on the stigma and prejudice ex-convicts continue to face even after serving their sentence. Being physically free does not mean society suddenly lets you start over. And what makes it uglier is seeing how some people can exploit desperation itself for personal gain. There are also enough funny moments throughout to keep all the guilt, anger, and heavy drama from becoming exhausting. The score swells and rises with the drama in the third act too, which helps the emotional payoff land harder. Well written, like Kita Kita (2017). What initially looks straightforward slowly reveals more emotional weight once the missing pieces arrive. It kind of reminds me of the quote: “Never be a prisoner of your past. It was just a lesson, not a life sentence.” Guilt, anger, and pain keep us in prison. That is why the whale shark imagery works really well for me. They may physically move around, but that does not automatically mean they are free. The same goes for these characters. There is no real freedom until they confront the guilt, anger, and pain that keep pulling them back into their past. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you've seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ While watching, I honestly kept thinking this was just a so-so film and deciding whether I would give it 3 or 3.5 stars. But little by little, the story gives me more information and everything starts falling into place. The flow and editing are really good because the film never gives me too much too early, but it also does not feel like the twist is forced for the sake of shock factor. When it finally happens, it lands hard and suddenly I empathize with the characters much more. The twist also gave me this stupid thought that Anthony Jennings basically became a double agent. But more importantly, it completely changes the emotional context of what came before. What I initially read as revenge starts looking more like people taking desperate measures just to finally confront the things they have been carrying. Available nationwide this November 25. Verdict: Near Absolute Watched at SM Megamall Cinema 2 during the Laya Premiere Night. Invited by Lunchbox PH. TL;DR: I am starting to appreciate Sigrid Andrea Bernardo's filmmaking more with every film I watch, especially how she uses framing and visual composition to communicate what her characters are going through. I initially thought this was going to be a fairly straightforward story, but I love how it gradually reveals something much more emotionally compelling without forcing its surprises. The performances are great across the board, with JC Santos easily being the standout for me. I also appreciate how the film explores the difficulty of moving forward when society continues to judge people by their past. The humor, editing, and score work really well together, and what I initially considered a decent film eventually became something much more affecting. I love it when a film gradually wins me over like this.
