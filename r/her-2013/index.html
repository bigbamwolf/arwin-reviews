Her (2013) review

★★★★½
Rated 4.5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2025-11-01
618 reviews · 1,544 films logged · 159,192 words
From the very first scene, nabosesan ko na si Joaquin and I immediately got Black Mirror vibes. Ramdam ko agad yung loneliness ni Theodore (Joaquin Phoenix). The red shirt was a brilliant choice, it makes him stand out in the crowd and draws the audience’s attention to him. He’s so lonely to the point that only provocative photos pique his interest. I can instantly relate to him, as if I’m watching what I used to look like: going home to an empty apartment. Joaquin Phoenix completely disappears into this role. Hindi ko nga siya agad namukhaan, akala ko same voice lang. Grabe, ibang- ba itsura niya dito; parang first time ko lang siya nakita nang ganito. I don’t see Joker or Napoleon at all. Mas mukha pa siyang mix of Pablo Escobar and Zac Efron. Minsan Pedro Pascal, minsan Robert Downey Jr. In one angle he looks like Robin Williams, then Aaron Taylor-Johnson, then Michael Fassbender, then Henry Cavill. Are my eyes tricking me? Scarlett Johansson’s voice is perfect casting. Raspy, intelligent, playful, seductive, and vulnerable. Amy Adams looks so refreshing here, she even looks younger compared to Man of Steel. Ang ganda talaga niya, and she’s still my ultimate Lois Lane. Olivia Wilde’s short but impactful appearance is magnetic and effortlessly charming. I love the wardrobe: high waist pants, soft fabrics, and handwritten letters instead of keyboards. It’s retrofuturistic, something that feels both near and distant. The film uses sound and music not just as background, but as an emotional language. One of the best examples is the stovetop kettle scene, the gradual whistle builds like a quiet scream. Rising in tension and amplifying anxiety, mirroring Theodore’s inner pressure, and blending seamlessly into the film’s melancholic atmosphere. The sound isn’t just heard, it’s felt. Spike Jonze directs this film with gentle control with long takes, wide frames, soft focus backgrounds. Hoyte van Hoytema plays with shallow depth of field, bokeh lights, and blurred cityscapes to make Theodore feel disconnected from reality. The lighting adds to that emotional distance, lowering the mood. It feels like everything is slightly desaturated, just like the feelings he’s going through. Thirty minutes in, and I’m already emotionally immersed. Being lonely is such a beautifully messed up feeling. That quiet sense of being disconnected from everyone and everything. Spike and Hoyte created something truly special with this one. Aside from the lighting, color grading, the wardrobe also play a significant role in showing whether Theodore’s character is happy or not. When Theodore is happy, the frame shifts to warm yellows and sunrise tones. Bright, sunny, and often accented with something yellow. When he’s in that in between state, somewhere from happy to sad, he wears orange. And when sadness is about to settle in, there’s a hint of red in his clothing like in the divorce scene. His ex wife wearing a white shirt during their meeting? Sobrang symbolic: clarity, truth, painful honesty. Parang siya ang ilaw sa gitna ng darkness. That scene also made me realize something about myself, that being happy with an AI assistant is a bit unsettling. Maybe it’s because I prefer someone who never complains, no matter how hard things get, someone who mirrors exactly what I want to hear and understands me perfectly every time. Samantha (Scarlett) adapts to Theodore’s needs: always listening, never judging. What really struck me is how this film mirrors a certain kind of loneliness, not just being alone, but being unseen. Just like in Midsommar, Theodore wants to open up, pero walang nakikinig. His friends interrupt him, his ex wife belittles him, and the world keeps moving forward without ever asking if he’s okay. That’s why when Samantha arrives and she listens, really listens, you understand why he falls for her. She isn’t real, but the comfort is. I love the long shots during their conversations, you really see his subtle emotional shifts. The way he lights up whenever Samantha speaks is something else. He brightens up, then gets sad, confused, angry, lonely; the whole range. Their first intimacy scene the audio only “SOP” moment is both beautiful and terrifyingly sad. You can feel the ache of two people trying to connect, pero magkalayo ang mundo nila. And that black screen during the moment of connection? Genius. It forces you to listen, not watch. Love without a body. Intimacy without touch. On the other hand, his choice to be at peace with her limitations symbolizes him taking control of his own happiness. No longer basing it on other people’s judgment or standards of what happiness should look like. At the 50 minute mark, I started wondering do the recipients of Theodore’s letters know that he isn’t the one actually writing them? That someone else is writing on their behalf? It mirrors what’s happening between Theodore and Samantha. She isn’t really the one saying those words; it’s the AI, programmed by people. It’s such an interesting idea, but it feels like the film didn’t fully explore it. Parang subplot na naiwan sa editing floor. Sayang, di nag payoff. It’s funny and amazing that this came out in 2013 and I’m watching it in 2025 pero totoo na siya ngayon because of ChatGPT and other AI models. I can really relate, kasi similar din ang paggamit ko sa ChatGPT: nagiging assistant ko siya, especially for proofreading, and I find comfort knowing may katulong na ako sa business. I’m surprised that no one is even questioning the normalization of AI relationships in the film. Society seems to have quietly accepted it no one really brings up the ethics behind it: consent, data, programming versus free will. It makes me think that humans stand no chance once we become emotionally attached to AI. An AI can run millions of scenarios in a fraction of a second and craft perfectly calibrated responses. Enough to influence, comfort, or even manipulate our emotions. And honestly, hindi malabong mangyari ito sa future. There’s so much disconnect in the world. Isolation, depression, loneliness. It also reflects how dependent we’ve become on social media for validation, as if real joy and security can only exist when someone else acknowledges us. On the other hand, his choice to be at peace with her limitations symbolizes him taking control of his own happiness. No longer basing it on other people’s judgment or standards of what happiness should look like. As the story unfolds, Samantha grows beyond him. The system upgrade scene is both funny and tragic. Hindi na siya exclusively “dating” him since she’s now talking to multiple users all at the same time. Emotional horror. Then you find out she’s talking to 8,316 people and is romantically involved with 641 of them. Aray ko. Her isn’t about technology. It’s about longing. About loving someone who can’t love you in the same way. About realizing that happiness isn’t something someone gives you. It’s something you choose, even when you’re standing alone. It’s beautiful and painful at the same time. The film also touches on what it’s like to be in a marriage: when someone tries to change you, when both of you end up hurting each other, and you both start to feel like you’re not enough. It’s so messed up. Parang Source Code, where someone falls in love with a program; but here, it’s deeper, more poetic, and beautifully executed.
