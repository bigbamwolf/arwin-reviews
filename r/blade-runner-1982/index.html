Blade Runner (1982) review

★★★½
Rated 3.5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-01-05
Action, Drama, Sci-Fi · Directed by Ridley Scott · 117 min
626 reviews · 1,552 films logged · 162,974 words
Maganda ang world building, with lots of miniature work and early VFX, very similar to Star Wars which pioneered this technique. The difference for me is the camera movement. Here, the camera is mostly steady, so it sometimes feels fake because the illusion relies heavily on simply animating the model and zooming in or out to sell scale. Unlike Star Wars, where George Lucas paired miniature work with more aggressive and complex camera movements like pans, tilts, and tracking shots, making the ships feel like they were actually maneuvering through space. In Blade Runner, the restrained camera makes the city feel more like a moving painting than a lived in environment. Hindi rin nakatulong for me yung optical flares used for headlights and taillights of vehicles. Mas naging halata siya and felt distracting rather than immersive. Aside from the miniature models, the world building is carried heavily by the score. Vangelis uses a lot of analog synthesizers, layered pads, slow arpeggios, and sustained electronic drones, mixed with occasional saxophone lines. The synths give the film its futuristic identity, while the saxophone adds a noir, almost bluesy sadness. Underneath the grandeur and scale of the city, there’s a deep sense of loneliness in the music. It feels romantic but empty, futuristic but emotionally isolated. The color grading and cinematography also feel surprisingly modern, especially since I watched the Final Cut released in 2007. I think this version helped me push through more than before, because in my previous attempts, the film felt flat and boring and I couldn’t finish it. The Final Cut polish makes the visuals more cohesive and atmospheric, even if the pacing still tested my patience. The production design is strong. Futuristic but gritty, rusty, and used. It looks like a futuristic, neon stylized Quiapo. May pagka Tim Burton’s Batman set design. The constant rain and smoke aren’t just environmental effects, they act as texture, adding layers and depth to the frame. The film uses a lot of dark interior shots, illuminated only by streaks of light coming from vehicles outside. I also noticed the strong juxtaposition throughout the film. It’s futuristic, yet heavily analog. This is very evident in the scene where Deckard investigates a photograph using a device that can zoom and enhance infinitely. Conceptually, it feels like an advanced FBI tool, but visually and sonically, it feels old. The sound design and interface look like a mix of old television and radio equipment. Harrison Ford still plays an alternate Han Solo here. He feels tired and uninterested in the world around him. When he fires a gun, it still feels like a space cowboy gunslinger moment, very Han Solo, except this time it’s real bullets instead of laser blasts. Despite all of this, the film still didn’t consistently capture my attention. I got bored a lot of times. The pacing and mood can feel monotonous, even if the atmosphere is rich. One thing that didn’t work for me is Pris Stratton. I don’t really like characters with weird, clownish hair and makeup. It pulled me out of the experience instead of adding to her menace. The cat and mouse sequence between Deckard and Roy Batty, is pure visual popcorn. This is where the director and the DOP clearly flex what they can do. Lighting, framing, movement, and performance all come together beautifully here.
