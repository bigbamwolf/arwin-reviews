The Witch (2015) review

★★½
Rated 2.5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-02-08
629 reviews · 1,555 films logged · 164,190 words
Non spoiler thoughts (summary): I’m locked in immediately by the voice and score, and the film never really lets go of that dread. The natural lighting and muted palette make everything feel raw and exposed, like I’m watching a world slowly rot under fear. The performances sell the paranoia, and the period detail adds weight to every interaction. For me, this plays less like a traditional horror film and more like a slow psychological unraveling where atmosphere and faith driven tension do all the heavy lifting. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you’ve seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ The film opens with Ralph Ineson’s thunderous voice layered with a rising creepy score and haunting female vocals, and I immediately feel the tone lock in. I read that most of the film was shot using natural available light, and I can really see that choice on screen. The visuals have this raw, grounded texture that reminds me of The Revenant. The dim candlelight, gray skies, and muted palette make everything feel cold and exposed, which adds to the unease. The old English dialogue, costume design, production sets, and cinematography all work together seamlessly. Every detail reinforces the time period and isolates the family from the world. The authenticity of the language makes conversations feel heavier and more ritualistic, like every word carries spiritual weight and fear. Harvey Scrimshaw’s performance genuinely unsettles me. I feel his terror and bliss, especially in the possession scenes where faith, fear, and confusion altogether. Ralph Ineson and Kate Dickie anchor the emotional tension with performances that feel real and desperate. Their parental authority slowly crumbles as paranoia takes over. By the end, Satan divides them, isolates them, and lets suspicion rot their bond until nothing is left. When Thomasin is alone, grieving, and stripped of everything, the offer feels less like temptation and more like escape. Overall, I experience The Witch as a slow psychological regression disguised as historical horror. It is less about jump scares and more about atmosphere, faith, and the fragility of family under fear.
