The Master (2012) review

★★½
Rated 2.5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2025-11-09
602 reviews · 1,528 films logged · 156,200 words
From the very first frame, the ocean waves churning behind a warship, symbolizes him being churned by a powerful force. Plus, the score is excellent. Hair and makeup are on point, and the wardrobe perfectly fits the period setting. Ang ganda din ng color grading. Yung intimate scene nila sa photo processing room ang ganda rin. Yellows, greens, pure solid blacks. Visually, ang rich and intentional ng bawat frame. All this careful world building would be meaningless without Joaquin Phoenix: his spine is bent like a question mark, his lips slightly twisted to the side. Joaquin Phoenix doesn’t just play the role, his entire body is broken in every frame. Joaquin is unhinged brilliance. His movements are animalistic, sexually deprived, mentally fractured. Even the way he runs feels like Joker: erratic, desperate, unhinged. He has episodes, breakdowns, hallucinations. You can already feel that same mentally ill Joker energy brewing inside him. I believe this is one of the reasons Todd Phillips cast him as Joker. Yung mga hallucinations niya, naked women walking around fully clothed rooms is not for comedy. It shows how his loneliness distorts intimacy. Philip Seymour Hoffman counters that with terrifying calm. A man who smiles while tightening the chains. And just when you think Joaquin is the storm at the center of this film, in walks the calm: Lancaster Dodd. Like some false prophet floating above the lost, Philip Seymour Hoffman enters both charming and eerily calm. The way he talks to Freddie isn’t loud or aggressive; it’s hypnotically compassionate. Controlled. Measured. He doesn’t demand loyalty, he seduces it. And that’s even more dangerous. This is a world without a God, only men trying to become one. That charm is the bait. But the hook comes later. During the processing scenes, there’s no score just silence, breathing, restrained voices. No music, no distractions. Just a man being slowly reprogrammed. It’s all dialogue, but it’s intense. The lighting adds to the mood parang ini-interrogate siya. The questions get more and more personal. Ramdam mong nasasaktan siya sa bawat tanong, pero the Master doesn’t care. He’s searching for cracks, looking for the exact place where he can break Joaquin and reshape him. And I find myself asking: what does it feel like to be on the receiving end of that? Are the followers truly enlightened? Or just desperate to belong? Pinipilit lang ba nila may masabi para makasali? Or talagang namamanipulate sila sa words? The cult system is brilliant and horrifying. You see obedience being built like a ritual: walk to the wall, then to the window, over and over, until your mind stops questioning why. It’s not about faith, it’s about control. Blind obedience. He gets really annoyed by the heckling. Goes to show that he really is just making all this up because even his loyal follower Helen (Laura Dern) saw inconsistencies. Which exposes everything. If you are truly enlightened, why are you threatened by questions? Bakit ka nagagalit when truth is uncomfortable? Hypocrisy quietly bleeds through his teachings. He preaches “no reaction,” but explodes when cornered. What makes it more haunting is Freddie sees it. The entire process feels like indoctrination: brainwashing disguised as healing. Pero kahit siya mismo ay mentally unstable, siya pa rin ang pinakaaware. He sees the madness in the cult more clearly than anyone else. Even Master’s son sees it too, but stays silent because he’s family, because belonging feels safer than questioning. That makes the cult even more tragic: it’s not faith, it’s fear of being alone. Freddie knows it’s insane. He sees through the lies. But he stays. Why? Because sometimes, belonging is more addictive than freedom. Even brainwashing can look like love when you’re starving for it. And mas lumala pa siya as he goes down the process. And the moment someone questions like when Freddie challenges the critic in the salon or when skeptics ask Dodd for proof, he loses composure. Parang mga cult ngayon, nagiging violent to the extent na pumapatay kapag may nageexpose sa kanila or nagsasalita ng masama against their leader, and they start wishing ill on those people and consider them as the enemy. And on the other side, Lancaster needs Freddie just as much. Their connection goes beyond leader and follower, it’s mutual addiction. One needs control. The other needs purpose. They’re not enemies. They’re two halves of the same wound. Visually, Paul Thomas Anderson frames them beautifully. The creamy bokeh only returns when Freddie is free from “The Cause.” Inside the cult, the lenses are flat: no depth, no softness, no intimacy. But the moment he escapes, whether it’s when he’s running or riding a bike across the desert, the shots turn painterly again. It mirrors the early boat scene under the bridge, the calm before the storm and now, this feels like the calm after surviving it. Colors breathe again. The contrast is stunning. It’s visual poetry that tells us Freddie only regains focus and clarity when he’s no longer under the Master’s grip. And that’s why The Master isn’t just about cults. It’s about the human hunger to be led. To be told what to do. To be seen. Kasi minsan, it’s easier to surrender your mind than to live alone with your thoughts. The Master doesn’t give answers. It just asks one quiet, disturbing question: If someone saves you from your loneliness but demands your soul in return… would you say no?
