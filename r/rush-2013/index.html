Rush (2013) review

★★½
Rated 2.5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-01-21
Biography, Drama, Sport · Directed by Ron Howard · 123 min
606 reviews · 1,532 films logged · 156,923 words
Daniel Brühl’s Austrian accent sounds weird to my ears, medyo distracting. But visually, the film immediately establishes a stylized approach. The pre-mental race visualization looks like memory fragments, almost nostalgic, supported by a highly stylized color grading that leans heavily into orange and teal. Because of that, the film sometimes feels closer to a Michael Bay aesthetic in terms of color and lighting rather than a typical Ron Howard movie. The racing itself didn’t feel as immersive as Joseph Kosinski’s F1. The shots are too shaky and the editing is too fast. Hindi pa nagsesettle yung visuals, nagcu-cut na agad sa next frame. Because of that, the film fails to fully place the audience inside the cockpit. There’s also very little sense of strategic racing. Walang malinaw na stakes during the races themselves, and it often feels like pure dramatization of ego rather than competition built on tactics, precision, and decision making. Instead, the film focuses more on their personal lives. This is less about the race and more about how Niki and James live, love, and self-destruct outside the track. If this were my first introduction to F1, I don’t think it would make me curious enough to explore the sport further. Sa second act, doon ko mas naramdaman si Ron Howard. The direction becomes more creative, especially emotionally. The hospital scene was especially hard to watch. Nadiri talaga ako when they had to vacuum Niki's lungs. Napapikit ako because it felt painful and invasive, parang lobotomy but through the mouth instead of the nose. This is where I really appreciated Daniel Brühl’s acting. His physical suffering and sold the gravity of what Niki was going through. The last race is the most cinematic sequence in the film. Heavy rain pouring down, strong atmosphere, and a powerful epic score. Every corner feels dangerous, and for the first time, the tension really works. Niki’s decision to quit the race shows real growth. He chooses survival and his wife over ego. In contrast, James still wants to push through no matter the cost. The third act works better than the first two despite the earlier slack, but overall, it still doesn’t reach the breathless, explosive, and celebratory payoff that Joseph Kosinski’s F1 delivered.
