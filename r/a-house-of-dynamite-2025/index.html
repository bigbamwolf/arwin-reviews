A House of Dynamite (2025) review

★★★½
Rated 3.5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2025-10-26
610 reviews · 1,536 films logged · 157,710 words
Parang The Office ang camera work: mockumentary style cinematography na may handheld shots, crash zooms, at sudden pans. It gives you that raw, documentary realism na parang may kasamang stress. You feel like you’re in the same room, witnessing every bad decision unfold. The movie shows that no matter how many times they practiced and drilled for it, pag actual na, lahat sila unprepared. Terrified pa rin, kasi tao pa rin sila. The early scenes feel chaotic: a lot of talking, confusion, down playing. Everyone thinks it’s just another drill. Then bam, nineteen minutes left to live. A lot could have been saved if bureaucracy didn’t slow them down. It’s frustrating to see how hesitation and endless confirmations can actually kill people. It makes you think, ilang real-life incidents kaya nangyari dahil lang sa ganitong sistema? The coin toss scene really hit me. Are they spending billions just for a fifty-fifty chance and zero assurance of security? Then comes the Secretary of Defense’s chapter, pure guilt and failure. When the time comes, there’s nothing left to defend. It’s a strong commentary on how fragile power becomes when the button is within reach. The film also dives deep into moral questions. Kailangan ba ng compassion sa innocent population ng kalaban, o mas dapat unahin ang paghiganti at pressure ng sariling bayan? That debate alone gave me chills. And the way the president jokes about the nuclear black book looking like a restaurant menu tapos the advisor calling it rare, medium rare, or well done dark humor at its finest. Comparing casualties to steak doneness? Grabe, that’s messed up brilliance right there. You can really tell how fragile the word trust becomes here. In the middle of war, “trust” feels like the weakest currency. Ang dali niyang masira. You can’t risk millions of lives over a single emotion or assumption. That’s the scariest part of the film not the bomb, but how thin human understanding really is. Character wise, this movie isn’t about spectacle. It’s a character study, showing possibilities of how people would react in a crisis they’ve only practiced on paper. When the real threat comes, even the calmest crumble. Everyone is terrified to their bones once the situation becomes real. What if no? A lot of wars really happen pala dahil sa miscommunication, lack of intel, and wrong assumptions about the enemy’s capabilities. Nakita ko rin dito kung gaano kaimportante ang espionage and proper intelligence gathering hindi lang para manalo, pero para maiwasan ang mga ganitong tragic misfires. Ang dami kasing namamatay dahil sa mga desisyong ginawa out of fear, not facts. The editing and pacing remind me of Weapons and early Tarantino films: divided by chapters, each showing a different POV. Part 1 is gripping, structured, and thought-provoking; it’s where the tension really works. Part 2 drags a little, as characters start repeating advice and over-debating decisions. Part 3 leans into personality dissection, focusing on the secretary of defense and president’s behavior. By that point, I found myself checking my phone. Even the wife subplot in Kenya felt useless. The movie starts thrilling and electrifying but ends anticlimactic. Still, I get it that’s the point. The story isn’t about the explosion; it’s about how human systems collapse before it even happens. The score helps a lot in sustaining the tension. I think it’s violin and percussion mixed to create that ticking anxiety. Rebecca Ferguson steals every scene she’s in. Short screen time, pero grabe ang range. You feel the weight in her silence more than the shouting of the others. And that ending it’s like a coin toss again. Will the warhead blow or not? Will the president let it happen to de-escalate or retaliate anyway? Pero the code is already given. The screen cuts to black. Bitin, yes, but haunting. Then there’s that powerful scene with the Retaliatory Strategy Advisor praying to God in his final moments. I hated how Idris, the president, mocked him, asking if God answered. But the advisor replies, “I am not asking for one.” That’s deep. That’s what prayer really is not asking for an answer, but surrendering, asking for mercy, seeking forgiveness in your last breath. The film’s layered symbolism forms a moral descent from faith to chance, exposing humanity’s fragile relationship with control. The faith scene, where the advisor prays silently while mocked by the president, represents man’s confrontation with mortality. Hindi siya dasal para humingi ng sagot, kundi dasal ng pagtanggap, an act of humility before inevitable destruction. It contrasts perfectly with the “menu” metaphor, kung saan parang restaurant order lang tratuhin ang retaliatory nuclear plan. Parang sinasampal tayo sa realidad kung gaano kadaling maging manhid kapag nasa posisyon ng kapangyarihan. This dark humor becomes a mirror of moral numbness, reducing millions of lives to “rare, medium rare, or well done.” Finally, the coin toss acts as the film’s closing symbol of fate a visual and thematic embodiment of how humanity’s greatest decisions often rely on randomness, pride, or ego rather than reason. Together, these symbols create a hierarchy of moral decay: from faith (spiritual surrender), to menu (ethical detachment), to coin toss (blind chance). It’s a chilling reflection of how civilization, when pushed to the brink, often replaces divine guidance with mechanical probability. TBH, ang dami kong theories kung sino mga involved sa inside job but wala rin pala hahaha sayang braincells. Naisip ko rin na baka katulad lang dun sa submarine scene sa Mission Impossible: Dead Reckoning na AI lang talaga yung may gawa nito and there was no actual nuclear weapon fired. Or baka test lang talaga ito an experiment to observe kung ano magiging reaksyon ng mga key people in power. I find that angle very interesting kasi it turns the entire event into a psychological and ethical simulation rather than a straightforward crisis.
