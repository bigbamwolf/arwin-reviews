Lawrence of Arabia (1962) review

★★★★
Rated 4/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-02-03
Adventure, Biography, Drama · Directed by David Lean · 227 min
601 reviews · 1,527 films logged · 156,111 words
Non spoiler thoughts (summary): Monumental in scale and craft. The film immediately impresses with its vast desert imagery, warm yellow and brown palette, and a swelling score that makes everything feel mythic and grand. The sense of scale is overwhelming, from the landscapes to the sheer number of people on screen. Wide compositions and patient pacing immerse you in the physical experience of the desert, the heat, exhaustion, and isolation. Even without fully committing emotionally at every point, the film’s ambition, visual authority, and thematic seriousness make it undeniable cinema. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you’ve seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ The film immediately pulled me in with the swelling score as the sun rises over the orange horizon, crossfading into vast desert landscapes within the first 20 minutes. Parang Dune. Beautiful. This is pure visual popcorn for me. My eyes are really sensitive to yellowish and brownish palettes like this, and the film just luxuriates in those colors. The scale is genuinely epic. The number of extras is mind blowing, to the point na mahirap isipin paano nila ginawa yun logistically. It’s already apparent early on, especially during the aerial bombing of the camp. Those planes looked real, flying at low altitude, and it’s honestly jaw dropping to imagine that being done practically. Alec Guinness still commands the screen, even younger and with black hair compared to the old Obi-Wan we’re used to. But the voice is unmistakable. May authority agad despite less screen time. Ang ganda ng wideshots during the journey to Aqaba. Those compositions balance the slow burn and keep the film engaging. The director does an excellent job immersing you in the experience. Ramdam mo yung init ng araw, yung uhaw, yung pagod. It really feels like you’re suffering alongside them in the desert. Lawrence, to me, starts as someone who defies the odds and leaves no one behind. He treats his comrades fairly and with dignity. “Nothing is written.” That optimism and idealism define him early on. But less than halfway through, wala pa rin akong cathartic moment. I started questioning him. Is he playing the Arabs or the English? Is he Aurens or Lawrence? This is where the film becomes a clear portrait of a man being changed by violence and power. After the intermission, medyo nagdrag na siya for me. The shots aren’t as striking as the first half, and some scenes feel more studio bound than the expansive outdoor photography earlier. But in a way, that visual grounding mirrors the story itself. From sunshine and romantic idealism, it becomes heavier and more constrained. The narrative shifts from optimism and self definition to disillusionment. Lawrence sees the ugliness of conquest and wants to return to who he really was. From something else, to somebody else. He reminds me a lot of Paul Atreides, constantly torn between morality and destiny, conflicted inside while wielding immense influence over people who see him as a savior. He becomes their Lisan al-Gaib. I can clearly see Auda Abu Tayi as a parallel to Stilgar, not just in appearance but in temperament. A kunsintidor, bloodthirsty, driven by conquest. Ali, on the other hand, feels like Chani, acting as the voice of conscience and restraint. By the end, Aurens goes fully bloodthirsty during the massacres. He loses his wits. He becomes what he once described the Arabs to be: barbarous and cruel. That transformation is tragic. Overall, Lawrence of Arabia is monumental in scale and craft. Even when it drags for me in the second half, its thematic weight, visual ambition, and psychological portrait of a man consumed by war and identity make it undeniable cinema.
