Collateral (2004) review

★★★★★
Rated 5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-01-18
607 reviews · 1,533 films logged · 157,261 words
This review contains spoilers after the non spoiler section. Non spoiler thoughts (summary): Collateral is a thriller that slowly shifts from calm to menace without ever fully releasing its grip. What begins with a deceptively gentle rhythm uses music, lighting, and movement to lull you into a false sense of safety before tightening the tension scene by scene. Performances are a major strength, especially the unsettling contrast between quiet charm and predatory intent. The film sustains pressure through controlled pacing, urban chaos, and morally charged dialogue, turning an ordinary night into something increasingly dangerous. It is gripping, stylish, and relentless, a story that thrives on atmosphere and escalating dread rather than spectacle alone. Spoilers ahead. Read on if you’ve seen the film. From here on, plot details are discussed. Jamie Foxx playing another character named Max? Sounds like a disaster! The piano score during the taxi rides feels almost like an elevator ride at first. Calm, serene, and non threatening. But that calmness is only for a brief moment before everything slowly turns dark. How often do you get to see Tom Cruise play a silver haired bad guy? And he looks cool doing it. Of course, it wouldn’t be a Tom Cruise movie without him running, but here that physicality feels more predatory than heroic. Javier Bardem looks different every time I see him. In this film, he’s calm, composed, and intimidating as a drug lord. Short appearance but effective presence. The tension is sustained almost all throughout the film. It’s a gripping story that never fully lets you relax. The color, movement, and chaos inside the Fever club immediately reminded me of John Wick 2. Neon lights, bodies moving, danger everywhere. Tom Cruise’s Vincent constantly denies accountability for his murders. His logic is cold and terrifying. People die every day. Everything and everyone is insignificant. Shit happens. I have to give credit to Jamie Foxx’s Max. He’s not just a passive hostage. He’s constantly trying to stop Vincent from committing more crimes, even when fear clearly has him by the throat. That moral resistance gives the film its backbone. I predicted that Fanning, played by Mark Ruffalo, would get shot. That felt inevitable. What I didn’t expect was the twist that Annie (Jada Pinkett Smith) would end up being Vincent’s final target of the night. That reveal raised the stakes for Max. The final sequence is pure Michael Mann masterclass. The cat and mouse action is clean, tense, and beautifully staged. The ending is very satisfying. A powerful conclusion to a night that spiraled completely out of control.
