The Whale (2022) review

★★★★
Rated 4/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2025-11-08
607 reviews · 1,533 films logged · 157,261 words
From the first frame, The Whale hides Charlie in shadows parang sinasabi agad: this is a man drowning in shame. The 4:3 aspect ratio traps him inside the frame, like even the screen is struggling to contain his body and grief. The grainy texture of the film adds weight. This isn’t polished pain, this is raw, sweaty, uncomfortable humanity. The apartment feels suffocating. Books everywhere, stains on walls, blinds always half closed. Yet, there’s warmth. You feel that he used to be loved here. Sadie Sink is a storm. Her anger is jagged, cruel, unforgiving but you understand it. Her father left her at 8 to be with his male student. She's not just angry, she feels abandoned, unloved, and forgotten. Makeup and prosthetics? Insanely well made! You don’t see latex, you see a body that has surrendered to guilt. His nurse/friend is not comic relief, she’s the only one who talks to him without pity or disgust. Her humor isn’t to make us laugh, it’s human survival inside unbearable sadness. This family is broken in different directions. Charlie is eating himself to death. His ex wife is drowning in alcohol. His daughter is burning in rage. Grief has turned each of them into something unrecognizable. Charlie eats when he’s angry at himself. It’s self destruction disguised as comfort. I get that. Sometimes when I’m angry, I do things that I know will backfire on me. We all have that tendency: to be self destructive. The score is subtle but haunting, piano and melancholy strings that rise when words fail. And the final scene, when he tries to stand, to walk toward his daughter, it’s not just physical movement, it’s him begging for forgiveness with his entire body. And yet if I’m honest, this film didn’t fully connect with my grief. Which I’m grateful for. Because it means I am no longer alone the way I used to be.
