All of Us Strangers (2023) review

★★★½
Rated 3.5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-11-17
659 reviews · 1,585 films logged · 177,479 words
Sherlock’s mind palace, but built out of grief. Beautifully lit during the emotional scenes. It feels like a painting came to life, especially with the warm interior lighting. Everything looks almost too perfect, like Adam is creating the exact version of home and family he wishes he could return to. I still can’t remove the image of Moriarty from Andrew Scott’s face. But slowly, I got to know Adam and his vulnerabilities, and that is when Moriarty started drifting away. He becomes this emotionally exposed, fragile man who seems completely alone inside his own head. His mind is really creative, like a true writer. He can daydream an entire sequence with his parents, complete with reactions, arguments, awkwardness, and conversations he probably wishes he could still have. It almost feels like a mind palace in Sherlock terms. But instead of using it to solve a crime, Adam uses it to construct the conversations and closure he never got to experience. Those scenes with his parents are probably the strongest part for me. He finally gets to tell them who he became, hear how they might react, argue with them, and receive the kind of acceptance he never had the chance to get. Claire Foy has that timeless beauty. I loved her in The Crown, and that astounding aura and charm are still here even as an ordinary citizen. She brings so much warmth and motherly presence. Her eyes tell a thousand words even when she barely says anything. The ghostly side of the film feels more sad and comforting than scary. There is something painful about getting these moments of love and closure while also knowing that they cannot truly stay. Eventually, it starts to feel like Inception (2010), where reality, memory, imagination, and grief begin folding into each other until Adam himself seems unsure which layer he is living in. And what makes it sadder is that he is basically healing alone. No one is really there to guide him through the grief or help him process all of this. Even when his mind gives him these beautiful conversations with his parents, he is still the one carrying everything. I love the cinematography, especially the warm interior lighting. It almost feels like a painting came to life. Andrew Scott and Claire Foy are excellent, and the scenes with Adam's parents are easily the strongest part for me. I wasn't consistently engaged, especially during the romance, and the ending didn't affect me as much as I expected. But there is something about Adam's quiet loneliness and the conversations he wishes he could still have with his parents that stayed with me. Verdict: Interesting TL;DR: I was drawn to how beautifully this film captures loneliness and grief through its cinematography, warm lighting, and almost dreamlike atmosphere. Andrew Scott gradually won me over with such a vulnerable performance that I eventually stopped associating him with Moriarty, while Claire Foy brought so much warmth to her role. I love how the film explores the conversations we wish we could still have with people we've lost, especially the things we never got to say or hear from them. The way it blurs memory, imagination, and reality makes those moments even more heartbreaking. I found myself much more invested in Adam's relationship with his parents than the romance, and what stayed with me most was the sadness of someone trying to find closure while carrying so much grief alone.
