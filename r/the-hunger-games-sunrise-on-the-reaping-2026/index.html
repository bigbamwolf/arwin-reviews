The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping (2026) review

★★★★
Rated 4/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-11-22
671 reviews · 1,596 films logged · 183,921 words
Haymitch ran so Katniss could walk. I can see here that Haymitch learned from Plutarch’s wise ideas about imagery. Knowing what Haymitch eventually teaches Katniss about sponsors, presentation, public perception, and making people care makes that connection even better. This is easily the most emotional film in the franchise for me, not because of one death, but because Haymitch just keeps losing people. The world building from the first half is still there too, not just in District 12 but also in the Capitol. I like getting another look at Panem before the arena because it makes Haymitch feel like an actual person with a life before he became the drunk mentor we already knew. Joseph Zada gets Woody Harrelson’s lisp, which is a nice touch, but I am more impressed by how he avoids turning the performance into a Woody Harrelson impersonation. There is a naivety to him at first. He is defiant and hopeful, and he genuinely thinks he can fight back. Then I slowly watch him realize that he cannot beat this system alone, so he learns to survive it and bide his time. That makes Joseph Zada’s Haymitch feel fresh while still making sense as the younger version of the man Woody eventually plays. Kelvin Harrison Jr.’s Beetee voice is also so on point! I can literally hear Jeffrey Wright whenever he speaks. Kieran Culkin is kind of not for me as Caesar Flickerman. He is way more energetic and does not have the same finesse Stanley Tucci eventually gives the character. Tucci’s Caesar has this controlled showmanship. He can be loud and theatrical, but there is still class underneath it. Kieran feels less reserved and more hyper. Maybe I can just read it as Caesar still being young in the industry and eventually learning a few tricks along the way before becoming the host we know. Maya Hawke is okay too. Nothing particularly new for me performance wise, but she is effortlessly gorgeous. She really looks like the perfect mix of Ethan Hawke and Uma Thurman. Elle Fanning comes much closer to making me believe I am watching a younger Effie. I love her here. So cute. She still has the Capitol energy, but she is not yet the full blown Effie we eventually meet. As for President Snow, Ralph Fiennes is still good, but I see Ralph Fiennes. I do not really see the younger version of Donald Sutherland’s Snow. The blonde hair is there, but I do not feel that same power and authority. If the performance is going to stay that close to the character we already know anyway, Kiefer Sutherland might as well have played him. At least the family resemblance would have been insane. Jesse Plemons is the one who gives me something genuinely new with Plutarch. This Plutarch still has fire inside him. He is emotionally invested, and I can see the anger. Philip Seymour Hoffman’s older Plutarch feels like the same man after decades of learning how to bury all of that behind calm authority, strategy, and calculation. And President Snow drinking milk made me laugh. There is something weirdly funny about dangerous villains drinking something as innocent as milk. Hans Landa does it in Inglourious Basterds (2009), Anton in No Country for Old Men (2007), while Alex and his droogs have their infamous milk in A Clockwork Orange (1971). My biggest problem is the arena. The expanded aspect ratio should make it more immersive, but somehow it did the opposite for me. The wider view just exposes how artificial some of the environment looks. There are so many flowers, what looks like ground replacement, and backgrounds that feel digitally replaced instead of raw and natural. The first The Hunger Games (2012) worked for me partly because the environments felt real and dirty. Here, the arena sometimes looks too CG. Funny enough, the non-IMAX shots actually look more cinematic to me. The compositions feel stronger, and the environments feel more authentic. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you've seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ Also, apparently this is Haymitch’s teeth origin story and Effie’s mahogany origin story. 😂 What really got me shaking my head was how the Capitol completely rewrites Haymitch’s story. He does something courageous, then they edit it, reframe it, and suddenly the courageous rebel becomes a savage victor. Betrayal of the century!!! That is such a dirty play because Haymitch wins the physical Games but the Capitol still controls the image of who won. And now I understand his alcoholism so much more. Before, drunk Haymitch could easily look like a cynical character quirk. Now I see the burden behind every bottle: his family, his friends, the people inside the arena, Lenore Dove, the betrayal, and the survivor’s guilt. Snow basically teaches him that anyone standing close enough to him can become collateral damage. No wonder he wants to drink enough to forget. Haymitch ran so Katniss could walk. Not because Haymitch personally finishes the rebellion. He carries the burden first, makes the sacrifices first, and learns how imagery and propaganda work first. Then Katniss eventually takes all of that anger and resistance and amplifies it into something Panem can no longer contain. That is why this reminds me of Rogue One (2016). Jyn Erso and her team do not get to personally end the tyranny. They push the rebellion far enough forward for the next generation to deliver the final blow. Haymitch actually lives long enough to see his victory, but Katniss’s generation is the one that finally finishes what people like him started. Rebellion takes time. Sometimes the people who start it are not the ones who get to finish it. Then that ending. What a beautiful surprise! After spending an entire film watching everything Haymitch loses, suddenly Woody Harrelson is back. Then Katniss. Then Peeta. The trio together again after all these years, talking about the Hunger Games as something that is actually over. No more reapings. No more tributes. No more Hunger Games. That hit me because this is basically the future he wanted. Haymitch spends most of his life carrying that hope through grief, alcohol, dead tributes, rebellion, and people around him constantly paying the price for his resistance. Katniss goes through the same thing later. Rebellion has consequences. Friends and family get dragged into it. People die because dictators do not punish only the rebel. They punish everyone the rebel loves. But Haymitch gets something that so many of the people he lost never got. He gets to see the other side. Snow is gone. The Games are gone. And remembering Lenore Dove after seeing that future finally exist got me. What a nice bittersweet ending. What a beautiful way to end Haymitch’s story. Verdict: Well Made Watched at SM Megamall IMAX TL;DR: I think this is easily the most emotional installment in the franchise for me. I love how it gives Haymitch so much more depth and makes me appreciate the character I already knew from the earlier films. Joseph Zada is excellent at portraying a younger Haymitch, and Jesse Plemons brings an interesting new dimension to Plutarch. I also enjoyed seeing how the younger versions of familiar characters gradually become the people we know, although some performances worked better for me than others. My biggest disappointment was the arena, where the excessive digital environments sometimes made the film look less convincing, especially in IMAX. But the storytelling, political manipulation, character development, and emotional payoff more than made up for it. I left with a much deeper appreciation for Haymitch and everything he endured. Well Made.
