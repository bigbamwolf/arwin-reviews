Pleasantville 1998

★★★★½
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-03-26
Non spoiler thoughts (summary): This as a clever film about waking up inside a reality that feels safe, controlled, and limited, then slowly realizing there is something more beyond it. What I like most is how it starts light, and playful, then gradually reveals bigger ideas without losing that sense of charm. The black and white visuals are gorgeous on their own. This film uses style to explore conformity, expression, and the fear of change. I do not read it as a simple right versus wrong argument. For me, it feels more like a reflection on how people define normalcy, how they react when that normalcy gets challenged, and how becoming more emotionally honest can change the way I see myself and the world. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you’ve seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ This really reminds me of The Truman Show with the idea of living inside a constructed TV reality. At the same time it has that WandaVision concept of being trapped inside a show without fully realizing it, then slowly breaking out of it. And it also feels like Free Guy where NPCs suddenly gain consciousness and start questioning their repetitive daily lives. All of them deal with the same core idea, waking up from a controlled and limited reality and realizing there is something more beyond it. The black and white visuals are really gorgeous. Then when color starts to appear, it clearly represents awakening. It feels like loss of innocence, stepping into a new life, breaking free from rigid norms, and finally being able to express real emotions. Color becomes a symbol of freedom. I also think the film touches on the division between conservative and progressive views. You can see how both sides perceive each other as uncomfortable or even wrong. For some, art and expression are natural and necessary. For others, those same things feel obscene or threatening. I do not think the film is saying one side is right and the other is wrong. It feels more like both are simply living according to what they believe is normal and what they think they deserve. And honestly, that theme still feels very relevant today. On a deeper level, it also works as a metaphor that people are not just black and white, not simply good or bad. We are complex, full of emotions, contradictions, and desires. The moment we stop suppressing those emotions, we add more color to ourselves and to the world around us. To break free, to be complicated, and to feel deeply is what makes us human. The scene where the repairman calls out David for eating the apple really feels like a reference to Adam and the idea of sin. Like the moment humanity gains awareness and is cast out of a perfect but controlled paradise. The courtroom scene also reminded me of To Kill a Mockingbird. The visual separation, the tension between groups, and how judgment is passed all feel very similar. There is that same uncomfortable reflection of how society divides people. What I like most is how the film starts light and fun, almost playful, then slowly transforms into something more thought provoking. It sneaks in its ideas until I realize I am no longer just watching a quirky story, but something that is quietly challenging how I see the world.
