Rocky (1976) review

★★★
Rated 3/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-01-19
Drama, Sport · Directed by John G. Avildsen · 120 min
609 reviews · 1,535 films logged · 157,567 words
This review contains spoilers after the non spoiler section. Non spoiler thoughts: Rocky feels less like a boxing movie and more like a character study about a guy who has spent his whole life settling. Rocky walks around with confidence and bravado, but underneath that is someone afraid to want more than survival. The pacing is slow and patient. Stallone plays Rocky awkward and stiff. What really anchors the film is the quiet emotional support around him, giving the story its heart. By the end, you root for him not because he’s the best, but because he finally stops playing it safe. Spoilers ahead. Read on if you’ve seen the film. From here on, plot details are discussed. Rocky has that big dick swagger energy, like someone who thinks he owns the room even when he clearly doesn’t. But beneath that bravado is a guy who has been playing it safe his whole life, settling for small sure win fights, small paychecks, and low expectations. The lack of a constant musical score makes the film feel dry for me. It’s harder to latch onto the emotion when the score only comes in during jogging or training sequences. Compared to films that use music to guide momentum, this approach feels restrained, almost too grounded. When the music does arrive, especially near the end, it finally delivers the emotional lift the film has been building toward. I personally prefer Sylvester Stallone as John Rambo. Less dialogue, more physical storytelling. Here, his acting feels stiff and dry at times, and the accent doesn’t help sell the emotional weight for me. Raging Bull, despite being heavier and more brutal, actually feels more kinetic and intense in comparison. Adrian, though, is a key emotional anchor. She’s good looking, yes, but more importantly, she helped him a lot to believe in himself and stop playing it safe. She gives Rocky something he never had before: quiet validation and permission to want more than survival. That belief is what pushes him to step into the ring not just to fight, but to prove something to himself. The groundwork for Rocky’s character is long, maybe too long for my attention span, but it does serve the payoff. By the final act, with the music finally swelling and the underdog narrative fully locked in, the film earns its emotional resolution. I end up rooting for Rocky not because he’s the best fighter, but because he finally chose to stop settling.
