Jay Kelly 2025

★★★★½
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-01-19
This review contains spoilers after the non spoiler section. Non spoiler thoughts (summary): Jay Kelly is a quiet, reflective character study about success, isolation, and the cost of living at a level far removed from ordinary life. The film strips away glamour to examine how excess can coexist with deep loneliness, and how achievement does not guarantee connection or fulfillment. Visually, the film is elegant, with lighting, color, and lens choices that mirror the characters’ internal states. It sits with regret, and the fear of realizing too late what truly matters. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you’ve seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ Jay Kelly played by George Clooney, is a star who wants to feel normal again. He has everything in excess and lives in a different reality, yet he is deeply alone, without real connection. He wants to self-destruct, not just out of recklessness, but as a way to regain control of his life and reconnect with what actually matters to him, his daughters and his father. There is a sense that destruction is the only way he knows how to feel something real or normal again. The film quietly reminds us that what looks glamorous on the surface is often anything but. There is a price to pay for a sunlit life, and that price is presence, and time with family. Once that realization hits, it often comes with regret, and the painful truth that there may be no do-over. That realization gives the film its emotional weight. This theme hits close to me. The fear of becoming too successful and losing sight of why you are doing things, or for whom you are doing them, runs through the story. That’s why sometimes I feel like I’m delaying my success, even if I could pursue it, because of this fear of not being able to handle and balance everything without sacrificing one part of my life. Ron, played by Adam Sandler, mirrors Jay in a quieter way. He is also a family man struggling to balance work and responsibility. In Ron’s eyes, he and Jay are the same. Their relationship feels transactional. If one goes down, the other follows. Friendship here is less about loyalty and more about mutual dependence. Visually, the film is beautifully shot. The color grading, lighting, and lens choices support the emotional tone. Everything feels controlled and reflective, matching the internal states of the characters. The technical execution reinforces the idea that this about inner conflict. I see you now Linus Sandgren! Excited for his work in Dune Part 3. Jay Kelly ultimately feels like a meditation on success, regret, and the fear of losing what truly matters while chasing what looks like everything.
