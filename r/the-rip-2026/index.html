The Rip (2026) review

★★★★½
Rated 4.5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-01-19
Action, Crime, Drama · Directed by Joe Carnahan · 113 min
600 reviews · 1,526 films logged · 155,998 words
This review contains spoilers after the non spoiler section. Non spoiler thoughts (summary): The Rip is a gritty crime drama that leans heavily on atmosphere, and moral tension. The visual execution is one of its strongest assets, with effective color grading, fog, smoke, and silhouette heavy compositions that give the film weight and menace. At its core, the story explores desperation rather than pure greed, placing characters in morally compromising situations that blur the line between right and wrong. The film constantly nudges the audience to question where the real threat lies, whether in the system, the people involved, or internal choices. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you’ve seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ All I could think about while watching was why didn’t they bring a money counter? If they were going to steal the cash anyway, why not just avoid counting it altogether? And why the hell were they walking casually in an open street without using cover? Some decisions really pulled me out of the realism. And honestly, Matt can’t be the bad guy, it just doesn’t sit right. That said, the visual execution is strong. The color grading works really well, with lighting accented by fog and smoke that adds texture and atmosphere. It gives the film a gritty, heavy feel that suits the moral tension of the story. The silhouette shots are especially effective. That moment where Ben Affleck casts a massive shadow toward Kyle Chandler is a powerful image. Menacing, dominant, and very intentional. A clear power shot that visually establishes threat and imbalance without dialogue. At its core, the story is about underpaid cops being presented with a temptation that could instantly make their lives easier. It’s not framed as outright greed, but as desperation slowly turning into compromise. You’re never fully sure who the real enemy is, whether it’s the system, the people around them, or themselves. One of the standout sequences is when they’re inside the armored vehicle, with lights passing on and off through the interior. It immediately reminded me of Heat by Michael Mann, especially the flickering runway lights at the airstrip. That same feeling of anticipation, containment, and impending consequence. The twist lands hard. This is one of those films where you think you already understand the situation, only to realize it goes much deeper. The layers unfold quietly, and by the time everything clicks, you’re already locked in.
