The Iron Claw (2023) review

★★★★½
Rated 4.5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2025-11-14
602 reviews · 1,528 films logged · 156,200 words
Iron Claw opens with explosive energy, a full on wrestling spectacle where Zac Efron appears on screen massively ripped! I feel that this family’s honor is rooted in prestige and the siblings are competing for it. Victory isn’t just a goal; it’s currency. It’s survival. It’s the only language their father ever taught them. There’s a deep contrast to the film. Greens and golds bleeding into each other. The sets and wardrobe lock you into the period, but what hits harder is how suffocating their world feels. Their father is exactly the kind of man who uses his children as tools to continue the legacy he failed to finish. Every wrestling match becomes a transaction, every achievement met with the expectation to do more. The film shows a parent turning their children into extensions of their own unfinished business. "You are Von Erichs," their father constantly implies, "so you must win." It’s really hard for people who grow up believing they’re cursed.It feels like you are destined for failure, with no room for growth. Zac’s training scenes are phenomenal. Hero shots that show discipline, drive, and the terrifying weight of being the family anchor. Their entire family dynamic is a portrait of dependency: needing their father’s approval so badly that they can’t do much with their own lives. The pain of comparison bleeds into everything. At pinagkaperahan pa talaga! In the last act, the film flexes its mastery with a beautifully executed one take shot. Perfectly blocked, perfectly lit, every emotion raw and exposed. Pressure looks like this. The pain of not being enough, even when you give your entire life for a father who never truly saw you. And then the afterlife reunion scene comes in, gentle and heartbreaking. A moment so beautifully simple. The brothers, finally free from expectations, meet in a place with no spotlight and no promoter. Just love. The final moments bring everything together. Zac’s character breaking free from the toxic rule that men don’t cry. That men must swallow everything until it kills them, is one of the most powerful shifts in the entire film. His kids telling him he can cry, and he doesn’t have to be “sir” or “champ” or “the next Von Erich legacy,” is the moment he finally becomes a real father. A different father. A healed father. A father who honors his brothers not by repeating the curse but by ending it. It’s the kind of love they never got to experience in life. The kind that isn’t earned through bruises or championships. He finds love and acceptance through his children, filling the hole in his heart from yearning for his brothers. Iron Claw is more than a wrestling movie. It’s a portrait of pressure, masculinity, and the casualties of living for someone else’s dream. It’s about sons who were raised like soldiers, not children.
