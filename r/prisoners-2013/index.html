Prisoners (2013) review

★★★★
Rated 4/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2025-11-11
626 reviews · 1,552 films logged · 162,974 words
From the first frame, you can already feel Denis Villeneuve’s fingerprints: the cold blue palette and the lonely silhouettes. That brown tinted cinematic look is Denis Villeneuve’s bread and butter. He uses it not just for aesthetics, but to trap you in a moral temperature. The earthy tones are his way of grounding mystery in realism, making every frame feel heavy, humid, and suffocating. And I know I’m in good hands dahil si Denis ito. Hugh’s acting is always good. You can see the exhaustion building under his eyes. He’s sleep deprived, desperate, terrified, clinging to the only thing he still understands: rage. If Arya were missing, I’d probably break the same way. He’s a father stripped of reason, driven only by instinct. As the film suggests, he’s one of the prisoners: locked inside his own anger. Imprisoned by his grief and anger, losing himself in the process. The film balances that chaos with control through Jake Gyllenhaal’s Detective Loki. Jake is another kind of captive: imprisoned by duty, by the pressure to solve the case and alleviate the parents’ anxiety. Paul Dano, meanwhile, doesn’t even need words. The way he shrieks in pain while hidden in shadows says everything. His silence is the film’s loudest sound. Denis surrounds his audience with silence, and low frequency tension that gives that crime mystery atmosphere. And then comes the twist. Nagkatotoo yung hula ko nung una pa lang, na yung nakita sa basement na bangkay ay somehow connected dun kay Mrs. Jones. And when it was finally revealed, it didn’t feel like a twist; it felt like a confirmation of something that Jake should have pieced together a few days ago. The ending, a literal car chase against time. And that final shot, when the whistle faintly echoes beneath the snow, is pure Villeneuve poetry. It’s ambiguous, haunting, perfect. Father and daughter trade places: one buried, one reborn.
