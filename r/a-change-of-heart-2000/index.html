A Change of Heart (2000) review

★★★★★
Rated 5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-11-28
676 reviews · 1,600 films logged · 187,473 words
Sometimes the heart remembers what the mind cannot. This was re-released this 2026, and I really wanted to watch it in the theater, but the limited screening times did not allow me to experience it. So here I am watching it on iWant. Oh, thank God it is available! Star studded. These faces are familiar to me because they basically feel like family too. I used to watch them every night in teleseryes when I was a kid, so seeing Gloria Romero, Johnny Delgado, Hilda Koronel, Dina Bonnevie, Edu Manzano, Marvin Agustin, Jericho Rosales, and Carol Banawa all together already gives the film this weirdly comforting familiarity. And the “Tanging Yaman” song hits straight to the heart. Such a solemn and heartbreaking song. Carol Banawa’s angelic voice carries so much emotion even before the story starts using it more directly. The film deals with money matters, lifestyle differences, unresolved trauma and grievances, and the effects of having a family member with Alzheimer’s disease. What I like is that the conflicts never feel disconnected from each other. Money becomes pride. Pride becomes resentment. Resentment gets passed down. Then illness suddenly forces everyone to confront things they probably would have kept avoiding for years. This really shows that not all rich families are happy and content just because they have an abundance of resources, and not everyone living in poverty is lacking in everything. Art’s side may have more material comfort, but that does not automatically give them peace. Danny’s side may have less, but there is still warmth and closeness that money cannot simply provide. The cinematography is simple yet cinematic. I think it comes down to the fundamentals. The framing, blocking, camera placement, and lighting are all controlled without calling too much attention to themselves. It still looks like a Filipino family drama, but one photographed with enough care that even conversations feel composed and thoughtfully staged rather than merely shot to be crossed off a shot list. Johnny Delgado’s performance feels so natural and believable. His breakdown after learning about Loleng’s Alzheimer’s hits straight to the heart, and his fight with Edu Manzano is so intense that I can literally see the veins popping out of his head. That kind of physical anger makes the resentment feel like it has been sitting inside him for years. Gloria Romero is always phenomenal. She can shift from lucid to confused to vulnerable without making Loleng feel like a collection of symptoms. She still feels like the center of the family even when her own memory is beginning to disappear. Hilda Koronel, Dina Bonnevie, Edu Manzano, Marvin Agustin, and Jericho Rosales are also great. Nobody feels like they are waiting around for their dramatic scene. Everyone has a place in the family and a reason for being there. I do not know why, but this does not feel like just another stereotypical family drama to me. It honestly feels like a masterpiece. Maybe because the script, performances, emotional build, and visual presentation all work together without making the family conflict feel manufactured. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ The handheld camera during the confrontation, with the tilted angles and floating movement, really works well in portraying the instability of everyone’s emotions. Once years of resentment finally come out, even the camera feels like it cannot sit still anymore. Carol Banawa’s voice gave me goosebumps and had me in tears when Loleng recognized Chona through her singing. Then Chona saying that her grandmother gave her strength during the audition makes it hit even harder. Loleng may already be losing names and faces, but Chona’s voice still reaches somewhere deeper. I got tears in my eyes again when Loleng thought Madeleine was Grace and spoke about the wedding dress. It is sad and happy at the same time because her Alzheimer’s accidentally opens a wound the family never properly closed. She thinks she is speaking to Grace, but what comes out feels like years of buried emotion about a daughter choosing love and leaving home. The lighting in Jericho Rosales’ tragic scene immediately reminded me of The Shawshank Redemption (1994). Both use rain, darkness, and a figure almost swallowed by the night to make the emotion feel physically overwhelming. But where the rain in Shawshank eventually becomes an image of liberation, here it feels tragic. The darkness closes around him instead of opening up. The film is also deeply spiritual when it comes to the relationship between God and His children. For me, God uses different situations to make them confront old wounds and mend broken feelings. The family keeps getting pushed into moments where pride becomes harder to hold onto than forgiveness. And once again, I was crying when Danny and Art reconciled and when Jericho’s character returned. Those scenes work because the film has already spent so much time establishing why these relationships broke in the first place. The music and score are also crafted really well. They know when to swell without doing all the emotional work for the actors. The performances are already carrying the pain, and the music just gives those moments the final push. The script is probably the biggest reason everything lands for me. All the characters are well fleshed out, and everyone serves a purpose. Nothing feels like filler. The inheritance dispute, sibling grievances, Loleng’s illness, differences in lifestyle, and the younger generation inheriting pieces of their parents’ conflict all eventually meet in the same emotional resolution. Everything comes full circle. The buildup of drama and tension was paid off. Perfect for a Mother’s Day marathon and Holy Week. Verdict: Absolute Cinema Watched on iWant. TL;DR: This is easily one of the most emotionally satisfying Filipino family dramas I have watched. Seeing so many familiar faces from my childhood already made the experience special, but the incredible performances, particularly from Johnny Delgado and Gloria Romero, made me genuinely invested in this family. I love how the film brings together financial struggles, generational resentment, illness, and faith without making any of the conflicts feel forced. The cinematography is beautifully composed, and Carol Banawa's voice alone is enough to give me goosebumps. I found myself crying multiple times because the script takes the time to establish meaningful relationships before delivering such powerful emotional moments. Everything feels earned, and I can see why this film deserves to be celebrated as a masterpiece of Filipino cinema.
