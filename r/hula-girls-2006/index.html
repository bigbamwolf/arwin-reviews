Hula Girls (2006) review

★★★★
Rated 4/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-11-24
673 reviews · 1,598 films logged · 184,900 words
Breaking tradition, one hip sway at a time! For me, this is about showing that women can and should have a choice in their own lives. This is funny with a touch of heart, and the crowd responded to it really well. I think that is the kind of combination Filipinos root for. That is where the "kiliti" is. Give us something funny, then make us care about the people underneath the comedy. It also has that inspiring mentor and mentee trope. She is so patient with them, even when they barely know what they are doing at the start. And the girls are open to learning. They keep showing up, keep practicing, and keep pushing themselves. It has that zero to hero kind of journey where the payoff only works because I saw the commitment and dedication behind it. I especially love Sayuri’s character development. She starts out timid, awkward, and clumsy, then gradually becomes more confident and committed. Seeing her keep going even when she looks like the least naturally gifted one makes her growth even more satisfying. I think this is also about breaking traditions and stigma, especially during that time and in a country that was so conservative. These girls were expected to blindly obey their parents and follow whatever future had already been decided for them. But they start choosing something for themselves instead. Women can choose what they want for their own lives and should not be dictated by what other people think they should be. The resistance from the town makes that even stronger because the conflict is not just about hula dancing. It is old ways versus a new future. The mining town is already changing whether people like it or not, and the girls become part of that change. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you've seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ I also love Kimiko’s mother’s character development. She goes from completely resisting what her daughter wants to finally realizing that Kimiko is capable of making her own choices. She does not just tolerate the change eventually. She actively helps protect it. That is why the palm trees work really well symbolically for me too. Hawaii literally cannot survive in this freezing town without help. New ideas are the same. Change cannot survive against tradition unless enough people finally decide to support it. And Christina, the palm tree, feels like a symbol of both the town and the change they are trying to introduce. Without attention, care, warmth, and love, it dies. The same goes for this new future they are trying to create. It needs the support of the community before it can actually blossom. The film literally has the townspeople bringing heaters to save the palms once the resistance starts changing into support. At first, this was honestly a mid film for me. But when the final payoff arrived, the long buildup suddenly felt justified. The mother finally accepts her daughter. The town finally starts supporting the change. Then the girls finally perform after all that training. That final dance works because it is not just choreography anymore. I already watched these girls start from zero, fail, practice, persevere, and slowly become actual performers. So when they finally pull it off, it feels earned. Well made story, characters, costumes, and choreography. Watched at SM North Cinema 3 during the Japanese Film Festival.
