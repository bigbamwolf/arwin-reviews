Jackie Brown (1997) review

★★★½
Rated 3.5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-02-07
Crime, Drama, Thriller · Directed by Quentin Tarantino · 154 min
601 reviews · 1,527 films logged · 156,111 words
Non spoiler thoughts (summary): I immediately felt Tarantino’s signature rhythm in how tension is built through conversations, framing, and quiet maneuvering instead of nonstop action. The split screen editing and power driven compositions kept me locked in, while the steady funk score gives the whole crime story a cool, controlled pulse. The performances bring a lot of personality, and the layered deception unfolds cleanly enough for me to enjoy piecing it together. I did miss a big explosive finale, less fireworks, more precision. A crime film that thrives on timing, personality, and quiet intelligence rather than spectacle. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you’ve seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ This is unmistakably Quentin Tarantino. Talk heavy scenes, long takes, and bursts of violence define the rhythm. Instead of chaos driving the film, the tension is built through conversations, glances, and quiet maneuvering. A great example is the split screen moment when Jackie stole the gun from Max. The timing is excellent. The editing builds tension by letting us see multiple perspectives at once, so we understand the danger before the characters fully act on it. It becomes less about surprise and more about anticipation. Tarantino uses framing throughout the film to highlight power dynamics. Who stands, who sits, who dominates the frame, and who looks cornered all subtly communicate shifting control without needing explicit dialogue. The performances add a lot of personality. Seeing Robert De Niro play a stoner is a first for me. His sluggish presence contrasts nicely with the sharper, more calculating characters around him. It’s also a surprise to see Bridget Fonda here, a nice coincidence seeing her after watching Henry Fonda in 12 Angry Men. Another coincidence i s Michael Keaton playing another "Ray" character just like in The Founder (Ray Kroc). The musical approach is one of the film’s strongest identity markers. The composer choices lean into repetition and groove driven tracks that function almost like a rhythmic heartbeat for the heist. The music rides a steady funk pulse. This gives the film a laid back but suspenseful tone, like a cool headed crime unfolding in slow motion. The repetition reinforces mood which makes the triple crossing scheme feel playful and clever. Narratively, Jackie’s layered deception is the core engine. The triple cross is executed cleanly, and the film carefully lets us piece together her strategy alongside the other players. Everyone thinks they’re ahead until Jackie proves she’s the smartest one in the room. That clarity of plotting is something I appreciate more here than in Pulp Fiction. The storytelling feels direct, focused, and character driven. My only hesitation is the lack of an explosive Tarantino style climax. The film deliberately avoids a massive violent payoff. I understand the intention, but part of me still expects that signature burst of chaos. Even so, I rate this higher than Pulp Fiction because the narrative is easier to follow and more cohesive. It feels like Tarantino refining his craft, especially in writing a strong female lead, something that clearly paved the way for Kill Bill.
