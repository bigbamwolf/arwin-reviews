Good Time (2017) review

★★★★
Rated 4/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-01-16
609 reviews · 1,535 films logged · 157,567 words
This review contains spoilers after the NON-SPOILER section. NON SPOILER THOUGHTS (SUMMARY): Good Time is a relentless, anxiety driven experience that throws you straight into chaos and never lets up. The Safdie Brothers use fast cuts, overlapping dialogue, and an aggressive electronic synth score to overwhelm rather than guide emotion, trapping you inside the tension. Neon lit nights, sickly colors, and harsh artificial lighting make the city feel hostile and suffocating, like a bad dream you cannot wake up from. The camera stays uncomfortably close, the pacing is merciless, and the sound design works hand in hand with the editing to keep your nerves shot. It is smaller in scale than Uncut Gems but just as effective in its own lane, delivering a masterclass in controlled chaos that is meant to exhaust, not comfort. Spoilers ahead. Read on if you’ve seen the film. From here on, plot details are discussed. Fast paced like Uncut Gems. Gets ko na yung style ng Safdie Brothers. Parang tinataranta at niraratrat nila yung utak mo gamit ang fast cuts, overlapping dialogue, at isang relentless electronic synth score. The music isn’t melodic in the usual sense. It’s more like pulsing, distorted synthesizers that feel mechanical and anxious, almost like a broken heartbeat. The score doesn’t guide emotion, it pressures you. It traps you inside the chaos. The film looks neon lit, especially at night, very reminiscent of Taxi Driver. The colors feel dirty and sickly. Greens, reds, and harsh whites dominate the frame, making the city feel hostile and suffocating. The lighting constantly feels artificial, like you’re stuck in a bad dream that never resets. Robert Pattinson looks completely ragged here. Laging puyat, pawis, half open yung mata. You can feel exhaustion in his body language alone. This genuinely feels like the worst night of his life, a nonstop chain of bad decisions piling on top of each other with no breathing room. The editing reinforces this. Walang pahinga. Just when you think something will calm down, another problem hits harder. Technically, ang ganda ng control nila sa pacing, lighting, and sound design. Everything works together to keep you anxious. The camera often stays close to the characters, invasive and uncomfortable, making you feel trapped with them. The electronic score, editing rhythm, and neon lighting form one aggressive sensory experience. Performance wise, solid ang buong cast. But the heart of the film is Nick. I really felt for him. He’s clearly being used by his brother, manipulated under the guise of “help” and “love,” while actually being prevented from getting real treatment. Connie thinks he’s protecting Nick, but in reality, he’s just dragging him deeper into chaos. Plot wise, it’s simpler compared to Uncut Gems. Mas maliit ang scale. Connie is just a small time criminal, not a businessman gambling with millions. That’s why I still prefer Uncut Gems overall. Mas complex, mas layered, mas mataas ang stakes. But Good Time is incredibly effective in its own lane. The ending gave the film a sense of hope. Nick finally becomes more self aware, facing the truth instead of being dragged around by his brother. It doesn’t feel triumphant, but it feels honest. A quiet release after two hours of pure stress. Overall, Good Time is a masterclass in controlled chaos. It’s not meant to be comfortable. It’s meant to overwhelm you, exhaust you, and make you feel trapped inside one terrible night. And it succeeds.
