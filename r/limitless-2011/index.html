Limitless 2011

★★★★½
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2025-11-09
Limitless begins at the end, like it’s telling us: this isn’t a success story. This is a warning. From that first scene, the film locks you in by visualizing cognition. The moment Eddie (Bradley Cooper) swallows the NZT pill, the world changes. The color grading shifts from muted grays turn into a sharp blues and glowing golds. Suddenly, everything becomes more vivid and every detail appears sharper. The film uses color and contrast to communicate to the audience if he is under the influence or not, especially with his eyes, which shift into an almost electric blue. And with that heightened perception comes control. He cleans his apartment with frightening precision. Every movement becomes calculated and clear. And at that point, the film makes you wonder hey, can I try that pill too? Visually, the film takes it even further. The camera performs that now iconic infinite dolly zoom or fractal push. Pushing forward through streets, sidewalks, taxis, walls, blocks, as if the world is expanding with his mind. Hindi lang sya visual effect it’s part of the storytelling.It’s trippy in the best way. But what really elevates it is how editing and psychology merge. Mafi-feel mo talaga yung disorientation parang lasing. Those blackout cuts: memory missing, time jumping, scenes skipped are exactly how addiction feels. You’re present, then gone. You’re in control, then lost. Parang Memento x Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind. And anchoring all this chaos is Bradley Cooper. His range is incredible here. He can be the smartest man in the room and then seconds later, the most broken junkie on the street. His performance isn’t about genius. It’s about obsession. When he’s on NZT, he is godlike. When he’s off it, he’s no one: shaking, sweating, lost. Then comes Carl Van Loon (Robert De Niro). He doesn’t need NZT. He is old money, old intellect. A power forged through time, experience, and discipline. His presence alone reminds you that artificial greatness will always be a cheap version of the real thing. So the film never really asks if NZT is good or bad. Instead, it asks something darker: What happens to someone who tastes perfection, then has to return to being human? Addiction here isn’t framed like drugs or alcohol, it’s addiction to power, to clarity, to control. And once you taste it, there is no going back. That’s the philosophy at the core, when you experience something beyond human capacity, it can lead to an inescapable kind of death. Because Limitless was never really about NZT. It’s about ambition without restraint. Intelligence without soul. Power without purpose. And the most dangerous addiction of all: the desire to never be human again.
