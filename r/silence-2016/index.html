Silence 2016

★★★★
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-01-11
From the very first frames, Silence announces itself visually. The mist rolling over mountains feels like a Japanese painting, almost sacred in its stillness. Different Martin Scorsese motifs here. The blue and green palette dominates the film, making the land feel cold, barren place, and spiritually distant. Ramdam mo yung spiritual isolation. There’s not much artsy experimentation here with lens choices or flashy shots. It’s more of a safe, restrained storytelling approach, but it asks very deep questions about faith and persecution. There’s an “Among Us” paranoia throughout: you never know who among the Japanese villagers might be a hidden informant or spy. The film also explores the root of Japan’s resistance to Christianity: not ignorance, but incompatibility with imposed belief. Natawa ako nung kumain sila, lamon agad yung mga priests, while yung mga Japanese ay mataimtim na nagdadasal to say grace sa food. It already hints at cultural and spiritual disconnect. Those priests who were raised in a comfortable environment, felt hungry after a long journey and suddenly forgot to pray before eating, while the new converts, who were marginally poor, did not even forget to pray first before their meal. Also one striking idea the film presents is that priests within the same faith do not respond to danger the same way. Even among men supposedly united by doctrine, fear fractures belief. Safety, survival, and responsibility pull them in different directions. I honestly think the converts or villager Christians would all be spared much suffering had they thought otherwise. That their faith in Jesus alone was enough, that forgiveness and salvation do not require priestly mediation. Because it seems that this whole mess is because of the priests evangelizing, and not the converts. The ending hints that the Inquisitor doesn’t care much about them, but only the roots, priests introducing Christianity to Japan. The film doesn’t answer this directly, but it clearly exposes how some churches complicate belief. I like the scene where Rodrigues (Andrew Garfield) is confused why the other prisoners are calm even in the face of death. It shows that sometimes priests, who are supposed to know the Bible by heart, forget its core teachings. The Bible repeatedly teaches one clear idea: dying for Christ is not defeat but victory. Earthly life is temporary, eternal life outweighs it. Scripture consistently says that losing one’s life for Christ is finding it, that death has no final power, that believers should not fear those who can only kill the body. Yet Andrew, despite knowing all this, is still afraid. That contrast is painful and revealing. That’s why priests, for me, are not very reliable when it comes to “walk the talk.” Verses like Matthew 16:25, Philippians 1:21, John 11:25–26, Revelation 2:10, Hebrews 2:14–15, and 1 Corinthians 15:54–55 all say the same thing in different voices: to die in Christ is not something to fear. It is something already conquered. The torture scene by the shore is brutal. Talagang hardcore test of faith under persecution, not only for the victims but for the priest forced to watch. The film asks several uncomfortable questions: How long will you keep your faith when God feels silent? Do you have the right to let others suffer for your beliefs? Liam Neeson as Father Ferreira reminds me of Ra’s al Ghul. Both start as mentors, firm believers in a higher cause. But over time, both reach a breaking point where idealism collapses under reality. Ferreira doesn’t suddenly stop believing because he is weak; he changes because he concludes that the system itself cannot survive unchanged. Both characters start as symbols of unwavering ideology, then evolve into pragmatists shaped by suffering and failure. Ang hirap ng problema ni Rodrigues. Will he give up the faith to save the people, or does he wait and trust that God will intervene? The scene where Christ speaks to Andrew and tells him to step on Him is powerful. Christ says He was born to share man’s pain, that He already suffered for all our sins. That even being stepped on is part of that suffering. This scene speaks volumes about Christ’s compassion versus Rodrigues’ pride. Christ’s message is not approval of apostasy, but a reminder that He already bore suffering. He already carried this pain. It reframes sacrifice not as heroic endurance, but as shared suffering. He is with us through it all. In the end, Rodrigues just learned how to play the game while keeping it safe. Keeping the silence of his faith alive deep inside him. He plays the system, outwardly compliant, inwardly faithful. This reminds me of Matthew 13:31–32, the mustard seed parable, where faith grows quietly and invisibly. One line that stuck to me was when Rodrigues said, “In my silence, I hear His voice.” It means that God’s silence is not abandonment. God may be speaking even when it feels like silence. It reminds me of the book of Job, one of the most painful books ever written. Job 33:14 says that God does speak, now one way, now another, though no one perceives it. Great acting by Andrew Garfield throughout the movie, though not as impactful for me as Hacksaw Ridge. Overall, Silence is not about finding God, but about what happens when God seems absent, and whether faith survives without reward, comfort, or clarity.
