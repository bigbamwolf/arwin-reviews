Lady Vengeance 2005

★★★★½
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-02-24
Non spoiler thoughts (summary): The slow deliberate pacing kept me distant at first, but the second half pulled me in once the visuals became more precise and the colors drained away. The film slowly shifts from stylized drama into moral confrontation, where memories and guilt carry more weight than violence itself. The real tension comes from watching people decide what kind of humans they want to be when given power over someone who destroyed their lives. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you’ve seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ I once again grew bored, maybe Korean films are really not for me. The pacing is very deliberate and slowburn, which makes it feel distant at first, but it got my attention every time she helped her cellmates get back at their oppressors. She is basically acting out vengeance on behalf of the helpless. After about an hour, the film suddenly felt more cinematic visually, almost as if the director changed the cinematographer halfway through. The compositions became cleaner and more intentional, and I also liked how the colors gradually faded away as the story prepared the audience for the dark main event. The desaturation feels like the world itself is losing innocence together with the characters. I am glad I gave it a chance because that shift makes the second half emotionally heavier. The slick black and white presentation works very well, and in the dining scene only the objects that remind them of their children keep their color. Those memories become the only remaining warmth in a life that has otherwise turned completely grey. The footage involving the murderer and the kids was honestly too much to bear. The acting of both the children and the parents felt disturbingly believable, just painfully real, which makes the horror land harder than any graphic imagery. And why the police imprisoned a wrong person? For me that is massive negligence. The story quietly shows how institutions sometimes prefer a convenient answer over the correct one, and that mistake becomes the root of every tragedy that follows. The choice Lee Geum-ja gave to the parents turns into a character study about what parents would actually do if given direct access to the murderer of their child. It reminded me of the moral debate structure of 12 Angry Men, where individuals challenge each other’s beliefs until a collective decision forms. I wish every child abuser and murderer would suffer the same consequences.
