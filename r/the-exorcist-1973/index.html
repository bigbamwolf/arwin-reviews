The Exorcist (1973) review

★★★★½
Rated 4.5/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-10-31
652 reviews · 1,578 films logged · 174,449 words
Fifty years later, I finally understand the reputation. I went in expecting to experience one of the scariest films ever made, but I ended up appreciating it more as a piece of filmmaking history. I was constantly noticing the decisions behind the camera, and that became just as interesting to me as the story itself. I also like that the film takes its subject seriously. It may not have terrified me the way I expected, but I can absolutely understand why it became such an important film in the genre. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you've seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ The cinematography is old school and I love it. The silhouette shots, the film noise, the framing, the wide shots, the camera movement. Even the framing and the push and pull of shots tell a story. The opening sequence in Iraq is probably my favorite part visually. I was just amazed by the photography, locations, ruins, silhouettes, and the way everything already feels ancient and ominous before the actual horror even begins. I can see why this is groundbreaking in the horror genre. It excels in all departments. More than actually scaring or disturbing me, I was mostly admiring the techniques and craftsmanship. I can only imagine how groundbreaking some of these techniques must have felt when this came out. The exorcism with the two priests is another highlight for me, especially from a practical effects and set standpoint. They actually refrigerated the bedroom set to around 20 degrees below zero so the actors’ breath would appear naturally on camera. Even Regan’s levitation involved physical rigs and wires. Seeing all those practical techniques work together inside one room is amazing. I also read Karras’ journey as someone slowly regaining his faith. He begins with his beliefs already shaken, then eventually reaches the point where he is willing to sacrifice himself. Chris begins from a much more skeptical place, so I also feel like everything she witnesses forces her to confront the possibility that there really are things beyond what medicine and rational explanations can answer. Merrin explains that the demon wants people to see themselves as ugly, animalistic, and unworthy of God’s love. I agree with this because in this modern world, when we feel like this toward ourselves or other people, then the devil already won. I think the ending is okay too. They may have defeated the possession, but it still feels open ended because the demon itself is still out there. Evil did not disappear just because this particular exorcism ended. The technical achievements were incredible, but the actual horror experience didn't fully deliver for me. Although I don't think that's necessarily the film's fault. I see demons in people's faces every day, so seeing them in a horror film doesn't really scare me anymore. I honestly cannot find anything particularly wrong with this film. I recognize it as a masterpiece, especially when it comes to cinematography, practical effects, and filmmaking techniques. But there is something about it that I admire more than I personally love. I can defend its greatness, but I cannot really call it one of my personal favorites. And I think that is what separates a 4.5-star film from a 5-star film for me. Verdict: Near Absolute Watched on HBO Max. TL;DR: Fifty years later, I finally understand the reputation. I went in expecting one of the scariest films ever made, but I ended up admiring the cinematography, framing, practical effects, and filmmaking techniques more than the horror itself. The opening in Iraq and the exorcism sequences were incredible, and I also appreciated the film’s exploration of faith, sacrifice, and evil. I honestly cannot find anything particularly wrong with it, but it never terrified me or left the emotional impact I needed to call it a personal favorite. I recognize it as a masterpiece, but there is a difference between admiring a film’s greatness and personally loving it. Near Absolute. Watched on HBO Max.
