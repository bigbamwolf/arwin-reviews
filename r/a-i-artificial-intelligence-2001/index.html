A.I. Artificial Intelligence (2001) review

★★★★
Rated 4/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-11-08
Adventure, Drama, Sci-Fi · Directed by Steven Spielberg · 146 min
652 reviews · 1,578 films logged · 174,449 words
David just wants what every child wants. To be loved back. I love the framing during the dining scene. David is confined and isolated beneath the circular lighting fixture above him. There is still a disconnect because they still treat him like a toy or an emotional replacement for their son, not a human being deserving of love. I also love the lighting at the start, when Monica is kept in the shadows because she does not fully accept him yet. Then there is the reflection on the folding door, multiplying David into layers. For me, it feels like a visual hint that there are still layers of him that have yet to be seen in plain sight. The film asks a really good question for me. Would I also love a Mecha child that almost looks exactly like my own child? If it looked, sounded, and behaved almost exactly like one of my children, would knowing that it was artificial really stop me from loving it? Twenty four minutes in, I was already almost crying when he called her “Mommy.” He is already emotionally attaching himself to someone who may never fully return that love. That imbalance is what makes David so heartbreaking to watch. Of course, this is Steven Spielberg, so the cinematography is undeniably great! Especially the camera movement, framing, lighting, and atmosphere of the compositions. The look reminds me of a mix of Minority Report (2002), War of the Worlds (2005), and some Blade Runner (1982) too. And the first two similarities are not accidental. A.I., Minority Report, and War of the Worlds were all shot by Janusz Kaminski, Spielberg’s longtime cinematographer. That explains a lot of the shared visual DNA in the cold light, shadows, reflections, haze, and the way Spielberg lets the camera move through a scene. Minority Report came only a year after A.I., while War of the Worlds came four years later, so all three also belong to the same early 2000s stretch of Spielberg and Kaminski experimenting with darker sci-fi imagery. Blade Runner is a different case since it was shot by Jordan Cronenweth, not Kaminski. But I can still see the resemblance in the cold futuristic atmosphere, reflective surfaces, artificial light, and the whole question of where humanity ends and artificial life begins. It also reminds me of I, Robot (2004) with the idea of AI self evolving, but this one has a lot more heart. I, Robot made me think about sentience and accountability. This one makes me think about what happens when an artificial being is programmed to need love. What happens if feelings of hope, dreams, wanting to be loved, and wanting to belong are implanted into artificial intelligence? At that point, the machine is not simply becoming smarter. It is becoming emotionally vulnerable. There is also this magical childlike wonder to David. Even when the world around him becomes cruel, he still looks at everything with this innocent belief that if he just does the right thing, maybe he can finally be loved like a real boy. John Williams adds so much to that emotion too. His score shifts between cold, eerie textures and more lyrical emotional themes, which fits the film perfectly because David exists between machine and child. Gotta praise Haley Joel Osment for a spectacular and heartwarming performance. The childlike innocence, emotional desperation, and the way he gradually feels less like a machine and more like a real child are all convincing. It comes through more in the stillness, the stare, and the way he clings to every little promise of love. I did not fully care for David emotionally at first, but by the third act, I really did. By then, his need to be loved stopped feeling like programming to me and started feeling like something painfully human. Verdict: Well Made TL;DR: I almost cried just 24 minutes into this film, and by the third act, I was genuinely emotionally invested in David. I love how Spielberg and Janusz Kaminski use framing, lighting, reflections, and camera movement to create such a beautiful yet emotionally cold futuristic world. Haley Joel Osment gives a spectacular performance, and John Williams’ score makes David’s longing for love even more heartbreaking. It reminded me of Minority Report (2002), War of the Worlds (2005), and Blade Runner (1982) visually, but this one has a much stronger emotional pull for me. It really made me question whether I could love an artificial child as much as my own.
