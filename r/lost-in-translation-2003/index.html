Lost in Translation (2003) review

★★★
Rated 3/5
Reviewed by Arwin Edward Bagaslao · 2026-02-04
Comedy, Drama · Directed by Sofia Coppola · 102 min
626 reviews · 1,552 films logged · 162,974 words
Non spoiler thoughts (summary): Quiet, awkward, and deeply human. Lost in Translation captures a very specific kind of loneliness, the kind that comes from being surrounded by people yet feeling unseen. The humor lands through confusion and cultural differences, while the tone stays gentle and reflective. It’s not plot driven. It’s mood driven. The film lives in pauses, glances, and conversations that don’t fully resolve, which makes it feel honest and intimate. ⚠️ Spoilers ahead. From here on, plot details are discussed. Read on if you’ve seen the film. If not, save this post and come back after. ⚠️ I got lost too, especially during the commercial production shoot scene. Nakakatawa yung chaos and confusion, pati yung “lip my stockings” moment. Again, I was lost. I didn’t know what was happening, and honestly, that felt intentional and funny at the same time. Scarlett has that very young, innocent look here. There’s a softness to her presence that makes her feel fragile and still figuring things out. I really felt Bob, played by Bill Murray, as a very tired movie star. He’s been doing this for a long time and he just wants to finish the contracts and go home. It reminded me so much of how I imagine talents or celebrities we shoot with in our business. Constantly being told what to do, where to stand, how to act, without fully understanding the purpose of the shoot. Maybe that’s why they get grumpy sometimes. What stood out to me is how alone he feels. If he’s a movie star, nasaan yung entourage niya? Why does it feel like he has no one with him, especially in a foreign country with a massive language barrier? Charlotte, on the other hand, is lost in a quieter way. She’s navigating married life, her sense of purpose, and what she’s supposed to be doing with her life. There’s a deep loneliness there. When she and Bob meet, they unintentionally build a safe space for each other. There’s clearly a warm connection and attraction, but more than romance, it feels like recognition. They both crave appreciation, understanding, and quality time, things they’re not getting from their spouses. Their bond feels intimate. It’s about companionship, being seen, and feeling less alone in a foreign place, both literally and emotionally. Personally, I would have preferred an ending closer to Past Lives rather than the ambiguous whisper and hopeful send off because come on, they’re both married. That tension between what they feel and what they could be will be the most painful part of the film. Overall, Lost in Translation really lives up to its title. It’s about being lost in language, culture, marriage, career, and life itself. Nothing is spelled out, and that’s both its charm.
